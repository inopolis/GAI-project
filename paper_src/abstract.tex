Repetition-suppressing decoders usually fix a risk budget at every step, whereas the quantity of interest is the probability that a loop occurs anywhere in the generated sequence. We ask why a fixed per-step budget can waste KL divergence from the model. A one-step controller has two dual parameters, the budget and its Lagrange multiplier, the price of risk. We show that the loop probability falls by the visit-weighted sum of per-step hazard reductions, that the cheapest allocation holds the price constant, and that a fixed budget instead implies prices that vary widely: to second order its excess cost is one plus the squared coefficient of variation of the implied prices. In exactly solved finite-state systems (eight settings, loop events of increasing persistence) a fixed price stays within 12.5\% of the minimum KL, whereas a fixed budget costs 1.3 to over 200 times the minimum, with and without infeasible budgets. On a character-level language model a fixed price reached zero observed loops at 39 bits per generation, a fixed budget at 1400 bits or more, and the budget rule realized per-step multipliers that were zero at most steps and in the hundreds at others. These are observed counts at one temperature, not guarantees; KL measures distortion, not text quality; and an estimated 98\% of every decoder's path KL lies beyond the cost of moving the loop probability, which is distance from a benchmark, not an available saving.
