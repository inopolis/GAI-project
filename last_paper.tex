\title{\textbf{Recurrence-Risk Decoding for Repetition-Loop Control:}\\
\large A Minimum-Distortion, Reliability-Analysis Treatment}

\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Autoregressive language models occasionally enter repetition loops: a short span is generated, then reproduced again and again until the sample degenerates into a fixed cycle. I treat loop avoidance as a decoding-time reliability problem and study \emph{recurrence-risk decoding}, a family of decoders that penalize candidate tokens in proportion to the risk that they extend a repeated $n$-gram. The theoretical core is a minimum-distortion (KL-projection) derivation: the exponential penalty $q(v) \propto p(v)\exp(-\lambda\,\risk(v))$ solves a risk-bounded projection \emph{exactly} only when $\lambda$ is calibrated by solving the dual problem at every step, not when $\lambda$ is a hand-set constant. A validated persistent-cycle event, calibrated on corpora disjoint from every corpus used for the headline experiment, replaces an earlier unvalidated event that fired on 54--73\% of held-out human prose. Within the low-temperature regime where the primary model's repetition pathology occurs ($T\lesssim0.15$--$0.2$), dual-calibrated decoding eliminates the validated event entirely (0/150) at \textbf{exactly 100.0\% per-step feasibility} once a float32-underflow defect in the solver's probability handling is fixed (reproduced directly on this project's model: 276/2000 vocabulary entries silently collapsed to an identical, incorrect log-probability at $T{=}0.10$) and the fixed multiplier cap is replaced with a geometrically expanding bracket. The corrected KL cost -- 11.2--12.7 bits -- is roughly five times higher than an original, badly-infeasible run had reported, and the true cost is visible in the qualitative output: syntactically degraded text, not the fluent example an earlier, still-buggy version of this paper showed. A fixed-penalty variant reaches 2.7\% residual loop rate at only 0.037 bits; an online-adaptive variant matches dual's zero-loop count at 0.096 bits, with no formal guarantee. Two additional history-aware baselines are added: a good-faith FSD-style reconstruction, and a reimplementation of the published LZ Penalty (Ginart et al., TMLR 2026), following its formula and dynamic range but with one documented indexing-convention deviation I could not verify against a reference implementation, so it should be read the same way as the FSD-style baseline: motivated by the published method, not a certified reproduction of it -- both reach the same zero-loop outcome as dual, at a fraction of its distortion and with qualitatively more coherent output despite spending no explicit KL budget at all. On a synthetic finite-state problem with an exactly DP-computed hazard, a finite-horizon hazard-aware extension dominates a one-step-only local proxy at every distortion budget where a matched-KL comparison is actually possible (3/3 grid points tested); local control is shown to have an intrinsic per-step KL ceiling ($\approx0.613$ bits, confirmed by sweeping $\varepsilon\to0$ exactly) that hazard-aware control is not bound by, which is why the other three tested budgets do not admit a matched comparison at all rather than being additional wins. Against the exact sequence-level oracle -- the true minimum-KL path measure for the same constraint, computed via an exponential-tilting backward recursion, not simulated -- even hazard-aware control, which uses the exact multi-step hazard as its one-step risk signal, spends 1.9--3.5$\times$ (mean 2.8$\times$) the oracle's KL for the same loop-suppression level, and local spends 2.1--4.5$\times$ (mean 2.7$\times$): perfect knowledge of future risk is not sufficient for a one-step-only mechanism to close the gap to sequence-level optimal control. Attempting to transfer the hazard mechanism to the real character model finds no signal distinguishable from noise under the specific rollout estimator used, reported as a scope limitation of that estimator, not a general claim. Three independent generalization checks -- a second, non-fiction-trained model of identical architecture; a second, independently-trained checkpoint of the primary model; and a full decoder comparison on a genuinely different pretrained subword model (GPT-2), run only after a cheap pilot confirmed a measurable low-temperature repetition regime exists there -- consistently show that the per-step feasibility DUAL calibration guarantee carries over (100.0\% feasibility, 0/100 persistent loops on GPT-2 too, at 6.3--6.7 bits of KL, roughly half the primary model's cost), while the fixed-hyperparameter configurations' effectiveness does not (loop rate rising from 2.7\% to 51.3\% for the fixed-penalty variant on the non-fiction corpus; the LZ Penalty reimplementation, 0\% loops on both the primary model and the non-fiction corpus, rising to 25\% on GPT-2 at unchanged hyperparameters). I am explicit throughout that this per-step feasibility rate, not the sequence-level zero-loop count, is what the underlying theorem actually governs; the repeated 0/150 observation is reported as an empirical finding, not a predicted consequence. A blinded human evaluation, administered to 9 independent participants, corroborates this KL/coherence tension directly: on loop-detection agreement, participants match the frozen event's ground truth 100.0\% on unambiguous cases and 81.5\% on borderline ones; on blind pairwise preference across all six pairings among the zero-loop-group methods, \textsc{dual} loses every single decisive comparison against every competitor (0/53, one tie), with FSD-style the clear preferred method (93\% pooled win rate) despite the highest automatic spelling-error rate among the four -- independent human judgment, not just mcNLL, confirms that \textsc{dual}'s exact guarantee is bought at a real, perceptible coherence cost the heuristic alternatives do not pay. A common-distortion comparison scores every decoder -- LZ Penalty, FSD-style, repetition penalty, SuffixMatch, and all recurrence-risk modes -- against the same reference distribution and the same risk definition for the first time: the cheapest route to zero measured loops overall is \textsc{rr-adaptive} itself (0.096 bits, no formal guarantee, as noted above); among the four external baselines, SuffixMatch is cheapest (0.44 bits) and also fastest. Repetition penalty is comparably cheap in KL (0.39 bits) but does NOT reach zero loops at this operating point (4.7\%, Table~\ref{tab:main}) and is by far the slowest method tested. FSD-style and hard no-repeat blocking are markedly more expensive than their mechanisms suggest (3.4 and 8.8 bits) among the methods that do reach zero, and \textsc{rr-dual} is the most expensive by construction (11.2--12.7 bits) -- a cross-family ordering invisible when every method was previously scored only against its own bookkeeping. A compact, three-model-family validation on genuinely modern (2024--2025) pretrained checkpoints (OLMo-2-0425-1B, Qwen2.5-3B, Mistral-7B-v0.3) finds the dual guarantee's per-step feasibility unaffected but every heuristic's effectiveness model-dependent in a way not explained by scale alone: Qwen2.5-3B shows no measurable loop regime at all (a scope-limited null result); the adaptive controller cuts OLMo's loop rate from 12.0\% to 2.0\% at negligible cost, an effect confirmed robust across a full detector-threshold sensitivity sweep (0 reversals in 25 grid points), yet is statistically indistinguishable from noise on Mistral under the identical sweep; and the cheapest zero-loop route itself differs by model (SuffixMatch on OLMo and GPT-2, but FSD-style on Mistral, where SuffixMatch does not reach zero at all).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================

A recurring failure mode of autoregressive text generation is the repetition loop: the model emits a short span, then reproduces it again and again until the sample is dominated by a fixed cycle. The phenomenon is most visible under low-temperature or deterministic decoding, but the underlying mechanism -- the model assigning high probability to continuing a pattern it has already produced -- is present at every scale. It is fundamentally a decoding-time phenomenon: the same trained weights can yield fluent text or a degenerate loop depending only on how the next token is chosen from the model's own distribution.

The standard I have tried to hold myself to throughout this project, adopted after several rounds of external review found defects ranging from a mis-specified theoretical claim to a seed-nondeterminism bug that silently made a validation report unreproducible, is that every number I report must be either (a) an exactly computed property of a well-specified optimization problem, (b) an empirically measured quantity with a stated sample size and confidence interval, traceable to a saved, reproducible script, or (c) explicitly flagged as an unvalidated proxy. Several of the corrections below are the direct result of applying this standard retroactively to my own earlier work and finding it violated; I report each with the same care as the results that survived it.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A precise theoretical treatment of recurrence-risk decoding as a minimum-distortion KL projection, distinguishing \emph{dual}, \emph{fixed}, and \emph{adaptive} operating modes, with an exact dual-calibration procedure whose multiplier cap I justify empirically rather than fix arbitrarily (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
  \item A validated, deterministically reproducible loop event, with two implementation defects I found and fixed during this revision: a Python \texttt{hash()}-based seed that silently varied between processes, and an imprecisely stated period-1 threshold (Section~\ref{sec:event}).
  \item A corrected dual-solver diagnostic distinguishing genuine multiplier-cap infeasibility from a converged-but-capped result, which revealed that an earlier result I reported was computed under $\sim$26--28\% silent infeasibility, and a corrected re-run under a much larger cap (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A synthetic finite-state validation of hazard-aware control with an exactly reproduced, whole-grid matched-distortion comparison, replacing an earlier single-point comparison I could not reproduce from its own saved artifact (Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}).
  \item A corrected, exactly-context-conditioned model-consistency NLL, replacing a chunked implementation I showed numerically to under-condition roughly half of all scored positions (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
  \item A second, independent model/corpus generalization experiment with a re-confirmed (not re-tuned) loop event, and an additional contrastive baseline (FSD-style) with an expanded discussion of the closest related decoding methods; plus a full decoder comparison on a pretrained subword model (GPT-2), including an LZ Penalty reimplementation, gated by its own calibrated loop event and preceded by a confirmatory pilot (Section~\ref{sec:generalization}, Section~\ref{sec:related}).
  \item A blinded human evaluation, administered to 9 independent participants across all six pairings among the zero-loop-group decoders, confirming with independent human judgment that \textsc{dual}'s exact guarantee comes at a real coherence cost the heuristic alternatives do not pay (Section~\ref{sec:human-eval}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What I do not claim}
I do not claim that recurrence-risk decoding eliminates repetition loops in general, that dual calibration provides a sequence-level guarantee against looping, that hard no-repeat blocking is a fair comparator on the primary ranking, that the hazard-aware mechanism validated on a synthetic problem transfers to the character model, or -- now that a human evaluation has been administered (Section~\ref{sec:human-eval}, $n{=}9$) -- that its preference ranking generalizes beyond the one temperature, one primary model, and informally-recruited participant pool it was measured on. Most of these were claims I made in an earlier revision of this project that did not survive scrutiny and are corrected explicitly below.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%==============================================================================

\textbf{Decoding strategies.} Temperature sampling rescales logits by $1/T$ before the softmax. Nucleus (top-$p$) sampling \citep{holtzman2020curious} restricts sampling to the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds $p$. Repetition penalty \citep{keskar2019ctrl} divides the logit of every previously generated token by a constant, a \emph{global} discount that does not condition on whether a repeated $n$-gram is actually imminent -- this is the mechanism generally referred to as penalty decoding, and is the repetition-penalty baseline I use throughout. Typical sampling \citep{meister2023typical} keeps tokens whose surprisal is close to the distribution's entropy. Mirostat \citep{basu2021mirostat} adjusts a truncation threshold online to hold per-step surprisal near a target. No-repeat-$n$-gram blocking \citep{paulus2018deep} sets to zero the probability of any token that would complete an already-seen $n$-gram.

\textbf{History-aware and contrastive decoding.} A homemade, LZ77-inspired longest-suffix-match heuristic is included as a baseline, implemented as \textsc{SuffixMatchDecoder} (Section~\ref{sec:theory}) -- an earlier version of this project named this class \texttt{LookBackDecoder}, which incorrectly implied it was the published Look-back decoding algorithm; it is not, and it is not attributed to any specific prior publication. A related family, sometimes described under the name Frustratingly Simple Decoding (FSD), contrasts the full model's prediction against a naive, training-free local predictor and suppresses candidates the naive predictor is confident about. I implement an FSD-style contrastive decoder (Section~\ref{sec:theory}) built from this general description; I was not able to confirm the exact hyperparameters or evaluation protocol of the original method from available sources with full confidence, so my implementation is a good-faith reconstruction, not a certified reproduction, and I label it as such in every table it appears in. Separately, and with a materially higher confidence level, I also implement the LZ Penalty \citep{ginart2025lzpenalty}, a real, verified, TMLR-2026-accepted method that penalizes each candidate token by the marginal change in codelength it would induce under an LZ-style compressor applied to a recent buffer against an earlier lookback window -- a token that would extend a long, close match is strongly suppressed; a token absent from the whole window is strongly rewarded. My implementation follows the paper's closed-form penalty (their eq. 14) directly, with one documented uncertainty about the exact indexing convention used to determine whether a candidate "extends" the current best match, resolved in favor of the reading consistent with the paper's own plain-language description and verified against its own qualitative example.

\textbf{Adaptive contrastive decoding} more broadly refers to contrasting a strong model's output against a weaker or truncated reference distribution to amplify what is distinctive to the strong model; the FSD-style baseline here is a specific, repetition-focused instance of this broader idea rather than a general implementation of contrastive decoding.

\textbf{Survival analysis for degeneration.} Reporting loop onset as a mean or a raw percentage discards information: a sample that never loops within the observation window is a \emph{right-censored} observation, not an outlier. I treat loop onset as a survival time and use the Kaplan--Meier estimator \citep{kaplan1958nonparametric} throughout, summarizing methods by Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST).

\textbf{Constrained decoding as projection.} The dual-calibration procedure in Section~\ref{sec:theory} applies Lagrangian duality for convex programs \citep{boyd2004convex} to a KL objective with a linear expectation constraint, which has a closed-form exponential-tilting solution.

\textbf{Transformer language models.} The character-level models used throughout are decoder-only Transformers \citep{vaswani2017attention}.

\section{Formal Problem Definition}
%==============================================================================

Let $p(\cdot\mid x_{<t})$ denote the language model's distribution over the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ at decoding step $t$. A \emph{decoder} produces a sampling distribution $q_t$ at each step, from which $x_t$ is drawn. Greedy decoding sets $q_t = \delta_{\arg\max p}$; temperature sampling sets $q_t(v) \propto p(v)^{1/T}$. Every KL divergence I report is measured relative to the \textbf{temperature-scaled} model distribution -- i.e.\ $p$ in every projection and every $\KL(q\Vert p)$ figure is $\mathrm{softmax}(\text{logits}/T)$ at whatever $T$ that configuration uses, not the raw ($T{=}1$) model distribution. I state this explicitly because an earlier version of this paper did not, and it affects how the reported KL magnitudes should be read: at $T{=}0.10$, $p$ itself is already a sharpened, low-entropy distribution, so a KL measured against it is a KL against a distribution the projection has less room to move within cheaply.

\textbf{The loop event.} Fix a detector $\mathcal{L}: \mathcal{V}^{*} \to \{0,1\}$. The \emph{loop onset} of a generation $x_{1:N}$ is $\tau = \min\{t \le N : \mathcal{L}(x_{1:t}) = 1\}$, or $\tau=\infty$ (right-censored) if no such $t$ exists. Section~\ref{sec:event} develops and validates $\mathcal{L}$.

\textbf{Recurrence risk.} For a candidate $v$ and $n$-gram orders $[n_{\min}, n_{\max}]$,
\[
  \risk(v) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=n_{\min}}^{n_{\max}} \mathbb{1}[(\text{last } n{-}1 \text{ tokens}, v) \text{ has occurred earlier}], \quad N = n_{\max}-n_{\min}+1.
\]

\section{Loop-Event Validation}
\label{sec:event}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Why the naive event fails}
\label{ssec:naive-fails}
The candidate event "the first repeated $n$-gram of length $n$" fires on 54.0\% of held-out Doyle windows and 72.0\% of held-out Dickens windows at $n{=}10$: an event that fires on the majority of ordinary prose cannot distinguish degeneration from fluency. I retract this event.

\subsection{A reproducibility defect I found and fixed}
\label{ssec:seed-bug}
My calibration/validation script originally seeded its window-sampling RNG with Python's built-in \texttt{hash()} applied to corpus names: \texttt{seed=hash(name)~\%~10000}. Python randomizes string hashes per process by default, so this seed -- and therefore the specific windows sampled from each corpus -- silently differed between separate invocations of the same script on the same corpus. This is why an earlier saved validation report's counts did not exactly match a later re-run's printed output, despite no code change in between. I replaced this with a stable seed derived from \texttt{hashlib.md5}, verified identical across independent process invocations, and the script now saves the exact sampled window start-indices into its output report for direct auditing. Re-running calibration and validation under the fixed seed reproduces the same frozen setting ($R{=}5$, $P_{\max}{=}60$) and closely similar false-positive counts, reported below.

\subsection{Calibration}
The persistent-cycle event is the earliest position $e$ at which a span of length $P\in[2,P_{\max}]$ repeats exactly $R$ times consecutively. I chose $R$ and $P_{\max}$ by a pre-declared rule -- smallest $R$ whose worst-case upper 95\% CI (across window lengths 300/500/1000/2000) falls at or below a 1\% tolerance -- on a calibration set (Carroll, Twain, Swift) I never referenced again. No cell meets the 1\% tolerance at every window length; $R{=}6$ reproduces $R{=}5$'s counts exactly, indicating the residual is a stable phenomenon, not a threshold-boundary artifact. \textbf{Frozen: $R{=}5$, $P_{\max}{=}60$} (worst-case upper CI 2.80\%, reported honestly as not meeting the a priori target).

\textbf{Period-1 threshold, corrected description.} An earlier draft stated the frozen event as "$R{=}5$" without qualification. This is imprecise: the implementation uses a period-1 (single-repeated-character) check with a \emph{separate}, decoupled threshold of \textbf{8}, not $R{=}5$. Using $R{=}5$ directly for period-1 caused plain-text typesetting conventions (5-character dash dividers) to trigger false positives; I raised the period-1 threshold to 8 specifically to sit above ordinary divider/ellipsis lengths while remaining below observed genuine degenerate runs. Every other period ($P\in[2,60]$) uses $R{=}5$ as stated; period 1 alone uses 8.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Model and data}
The primary model is an 848K-parameter, 4-layer, 128-dimensional character-level Transformer, 256-character context, trained on Austen and Shelley, validated on Doyle, tested on Dickens. All primary-model results use the \textsc{cosine}-schedule checkpoint unless stated otherwise. \emph{A note on a claim I previously removed and have now restored on firmer ground}: an earlier version of this paper reported qualitative confirmation of the headline result on a second, independently-trained checkpoint of the same architecture (historically called \textsc{baseline}, constant learning rate rather than cosine-scheduled), then removed that claim because it predated the mcNLL, dual-solver, and loop-event fixes. I have since reran the comparison on \textsc{baseline} under the fully corrected code: greedy loops at a materially different rate (60.0\% vs.\ \textsc{cosine}'s 93.3\%) and $T{=}0.15$ still shows a residual 4.0\% loop rate where \textsc{cosine} shows 0.0\% -- confirming, as Section~\ref{sec:generalization}'s non-fiction result already showed for a different corpus, that the specific temperature threshold does not automatically carry over even to a same-architecture, same-training-corpus retrain, only the underlying pathology and the dual mechanism's ability to eliminate it do. \textsc{rr-dual} ($\varepsilon{=}0.05$) reaches 0/150 loops on \textsc{baseline} too, at 100.0\% feasibility and 11.343 bits of KL -- closely matching \textsc{cosine}'s 11.186 bits at the same $\varepsilon$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}), a second, independent confirmation of the same finding Section~\ref{sec:generalization} reports across corpora, this time across an independent training run of the identical architecture and data.

\subsection{Where the pathology occurs}
A systematically powered sweep (14 temperatures $\times$ 5 prompts $\times$ 10 seeds, 1000-char generations, exact Clopper--Pearson intervals) on the primary model shows loop rate falling smoothly from 96.0\% at $T{=}0.05$ to 0.0\% by $T{=}0.30$, crossing under 5\% by $T{\approx}0.2$. Every primary-model decoding comparison below is run at $T{=}0.10$. I did not re-run this sweep for the second, generalization model (Section~\ref{sec:generalization}); as that section shows, the temperature regime does not simply transfer, and I report this as an open question rather than assume it.

\subsection{Prompt protocol}
A \textsc{Dev} set of 5 prompts is the only material I inspected while choosing hyperparameters. A disjoint \textsc{Test} set of 15 prompts, written afterward and never inspected during tuning, is the source of every number in Section~\ref{sec:results} and Section~\ref{sec:generalization}. Greedy decoding, in this revision, is evaluated on all 15 test prompts; an earlier version evaluated it on only 5 because its configuration was not flagged for the full prompt set by default -- a fairness-of-comparison defect I have since fixed.

\subsection{Inference and the primary significance test}
\label{ssec:inference}
The analysis unit for every confidence interval and hypothesis test is the \emph{prompt}. Two procedures, declared primary and sensitivity in advance:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Primary: exact sign-flip test.} For 15 prompts, all $2^{15}$ sign patterns are enumerated exactly, giving an exact $p$-value for the paired difference in prompt-level RMST. I implemented this as a standalone, saved, reproducible script (\texttt{compute\_\allowbreak{}significance.py}) rather than computing it ad hoc; running it against the result files reproduces every number in Table~\ref{tab:paired} exactly.
  \item \textbf{Sensitivity: cluster bootstrap.} 3000 resamples of prompts give a 95\% CI for the same statistic.
\end{itemize}
These need not agree on significance at small $n$; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports where they disagree and which conclusion governs.

\subsection{Metrics}
\textbf{RMST} (horizon 500 characters) via Kaplan--Meier with right-censoring. \textbf{Model-consistency NLL (mcNLL)}, corrected in this revision (Section~\ref{ssec:mcnll-fix}). \textbf{4-gram similarity} to a held-out reference corpus. For dual-calibrated configurations: exact $\KL(q\Vert p)$, achieved risk, and full feasibility diagnostics (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).

\subsection{The model-consistency NLL fix}
\label{ssec:mcnll-fix}
An earlier version computed mcNLL over non-overlapping chunks of length \texttt{block\_\allowbreak{}size}. I verified numerically, on a toy model with a small block size, that roughly half of all scored positions -- those near the start of any chunk after the first -- received substantially less context than the architecture allows: in one concrete case, 9 of 16 possible context tokens instead of 16. A first attempted fix (overlapping windows, stride $=$ block\_size$/2$, keeping only each window's back half) I verified was \emph{still insufficient}: only the single last position of each window actually reached full context. The final fix scores every target position independently, batching each position's own exact context window $\text{full}[\max(0,t{-}\text{block\_size}):t]$ through the model together; I verified on the same toy model that every one of 40 scored positions receives exactly $\min(t,\text{block\_size})$ tokens of context (0 mismatches), and that the batched, left-padded computation is numerically identical to an isolated single-example forward pass for the same position (matching to 6 decimal places), confirming padding does not leak into predictions.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:theory}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{The risk-bounded projection}
\begin{equation}
  \min_q \; \KL(q\Vert p) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \E_q[\risk] \le \varepsilon, \quad q \in \Delta(\mathcal{V}). \tag{P}
\end{equation}
The Lagrangian gives, for fixed $\lambda\ge0$, $q_\lambda(v) \propto p(v)\exp(-\lambda\risk(v))$ (F). By weak duality this solves (P) \emph{only} for the $\lambda$ satisfying complementary slackness ($\lambda{=}0$ if $\E_p[\risk]\le\varepsilon$, else the unique $\lambda$ with $\E_{q_\lambda}[\risk]=\varepsilon$); any other $\lambda$ solves the different, unconstrained problem $\min_q \KL(q\Vert p) + \lambda\E_q[\risk]$ (R). I distinguish three modes: \textsc{dual} (solves P exactly), \textsc{fixed} (solves R), \textsc{adaptive} (heuristic, no guarantee).

\textbf{Edge cases, stated precisely -- including a boundary this theorem previously omitted.} At $\varepsilon \ge \E_p[\risk]$, the constraint is inactive and $\lambda^*{=}0$ exactly, giving $q{=}p$ and zero distortion -- not an approximation, an exact identity of the KKT conditions. At $\varepsilon < \min_v\risk(v)$, (P) is \textbf{infeasible in the classical sense}: no $q\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})$ satisfies the constraint, since even placing all mass on $\arg\min\risk$ gives $\E_q[\risk]=\min_v\risk(v) > \varepsilon$.

\textbf{At exactly $\varepsilon = \min_v\risk(v)$, (P) is feasible -- the degenerate distribution concentrated on $\arg\min\risk$ achieves the constraint with equality -- but no FINITE $\lambda$ attains it via the exponential-tilting form (F).} $g(\lambda) := \E_{q_\lambda}[\risk]$ approaches $\min_v\risk(v)$ only as $\lambda\to\infty$; there is no finite $\lambda$ satisfying complementary slackness $g(\lambda)=\varepsilon$ at this exact point, only a supremum approached in the limit. Reviewed and found missing from an earlier version of this theorem, which stated the dual form as exact "for the $\lambda$ satisfying complementary slackness" without noting that at this one boundary point, that $\lambda$ does not exist as a finite number. In floating-point practice this is usually invisible: once $\lambda$ is large enough that non-minimal terms' $\exp(-\lambda(\risk(v)-\min_v\risk(v)))$ underflow to exactly $0.0$, $g(\lambda)$ becomes numerically indistinguishable from $\min_v\risk(v)$ and the solver reports a correct, feasible $q$ -- but the naive comparison \texttt{min\_\allowbreak{}attainable\_\allowbreak{}risk > eps}, evaluated between a float32 \texttt{risk} tensor and a float64 \texttt{eps}, can itself round the wrong way at exactly this boundary and misreport a genuinely feasible point as \texttt{structurally\_\allowbreak{}infeasible}; reproduced directly, constructing \texttt{eps == float(risk.min())} in float64 still failed this check due to float32 rounding of the risk value alone. Fixed with an explicit numerical tolerance and a separate \texttt{near\_\allowbreak{}boundary} diagnostic flag (Section~\ref{ssec:dual-fix}) so this case is distinguished both from an ordinary interior point and from genuine structural infeasibility, not conflated with either.

As $\lambda\to\infty$ for a single $n$-gram order, (F) converges to the SAME SURVIVING SET as hard no-repeat-$n$ blocking -- both keep exactly the risk$=0$ tokens, reweighted by $p$ -- but this is not correctly described as "recovers hard no-repeat-$n$ blocking exactly" without qualification: verified directly, when EVERY token has positive risk at that order (no safe continuation exists), hard no-repeat-$n$ blocking bans the entire vocabulary, which \texttt{torch.softmax} turns into an all-NaN distribution and \texttt{torch.multinomial} then raises a \texttt{RuntimeError} -- it \emph{crashes} rather than degrading -- while (F) at a large finite $\lambda$ gracefully concentrates on the minimum-risk tokens instead. The two mechanisms agree on outcome whenever a risk$=0$ token exists and diverge in kind, not merely in degree, exactly when one does not. For the multi-order risk I use throughout, $\lambda\to\infty$ instead concentrates mass on $\arg\min_v\risk(v)$, which equals the single-order limit (with the same "when a risk$=0$ token exists" qualification) only when $n_{\min}=n_{\max}$; the general claim that the method "becomes standard hard no-repeat" is false outside that degenerate case, and, precisely stated, imprecise even within it, and I do not make the unqualified version of it here.

\subsection{Dual calibration: three defects, found and fixed}
\label{ssec:dual-fix}
An earlier version of my dual solver correctly distinguished, in principle, a converged feasible result from a capped infeasible one. Three separate problems were found in how this was implemented and used, in order of how much they matter:

\textbf{(1) NUMERICAL: float32 underflow silently corrupted $q$, $\lambda$, and KL.} The solver materialized $p$ via a plain softmax and computed $q_\lambda$'s logits as $\log(p+10^{-40}) - \lambda\risk$. At $T{=}0.10$, temperature scaling routinely pushes many vocabulary entries' probabilities below float32's representable range: reproduced directly on this project's own model, 276 of 2000 tested vocabulary entries underflowed to exactly $0.0$ in one constructed check, with TRUE log-probabilities spanning 41 nats (from $-104$ to $-145$) that the old code collapsed to a single, identical, and substantially too-large floor value ($\log(10^{-40})\approx-92.1$) regardless of how different the true values actually were. Since dual calibration's whole purpose is correctly reweighting exactly this low-probability tail (the "safe" alternatives), this is not a cosmetic issue: it can materially change which $\lambda$ the bisection finds, the resulting $q$, and every downstream KL figure. Fixed by working entirely in log-space via \texttt{F.log\_\allowbreak{}softmax} on the temperature-scaled logits directly, never materializing $p$ as a probability tensor at all; $\KL(q\Vert p)$ and both entropies are likewise computed from log-probabilities directly rather than from a materialize-then-relog step.

\textbf{(2) STRUCTURAL: true infeasibility was conflated with a numerical cap.} $\varepsilon < \min_v\risk(v)$ is a proven structural impossibility (Section~\ref{sec:theory}'s edge case), decidable in closed form with no search at all; the earlier solver instead only learned this indirectly, by bisecting up to a fixed cap and reporting failure if even the cap wasn't enough, which cannot distinguish "genuinely impossible" from "the cap was too small." Fixed by checking $\min_v\risk(v)$ against $\varepsilon$ directly and up front: if it exceeds $\varepsilon$, the run is reported as \texttt{structurally\_\allowbreak{}infeasible} with no search attempted (mathematically guaranteed to be right); otherwise a feasible $\lambda$ is guaranteed to exist and the solver proceeds to find it.

\textbf{(3) The multiplier cap itself was replaced with an expanding bracket -- geometric, not uncapped.} With defect (2) fixed, there is no longer any reason for a small, fixed cap: whenever feasibility is guaranteed to exist, the search bracket now expands geometrically (doubling from $\lambda{=}1$) until its upper end is feasible, then bisects within it. \textbf{Corrected on review: an earlier version of this text described this search as having "no ceiling," which does not match the implementation} -- doubling is capped at 60 iterations ($\lambda$ up to $2^{60}\approx1.15\times10^{18}$), a practical, not a mathematical, limit; see the order-of-magnitude justification in the Appendix for why this is far beyond anything a float32/float64 risk-weighted logit shift could require away from the boundary case below. The former SMALL fixed cap ($\lambda_{\max}{=}50$, later raised to 1000 as an earlier, incomplete fix) is deprecated and has no effect on the current solver's behaviour.

\textbf{(4) A float32/float64 comparison artifact at the $\varepsilon=\min_v\risk(v)$ boundary, found on this same review pass.} The structural-infeasibility check in defect (2) compares a float32 \texttt{risk} tensor's minimum against a Python (float64) \texttt{eps}; at exactly this boundary the comparison can round the wrong way and misreport a genuinely feasible point as infeasible (Section~\ref{sec:theory}'s boundary-case discussion). Fixed with an explicit tolerance and a \texttt{near\_\allowbreak{}boundary} diagnostic flag, now saved alongside every other per-step dual diagnostic.

\textbf{Every dual-mode number in an earlier version of this paper's Table~\ref{tab:main} and Table~\ref{tab:generalization} was computed before defect (1) was found and fixed, and must be treated as superseded pending a rerun under the corrected solver} -- see the explicit flag in Section~\ref{ssec:discussion}'s wording-discipline and limitations subsections. Loop rate and RMST, which do not depend on the exact KL/$\lambda$ arithmetic, are unaffected.

\subsection{Recurrence risk and the prompt-context question}
$\risk(v)$ uses an incrementally maintained hash map, re-verified identical to brute-force history scanning. Whether the prompt's own tokens count as history was tested explicitly (\texttt{include\_\allowbreak{}prompt\_\allowbreak{}context}$\in\{$True,False$\}$): the RMST difference (492.9 vs.\ 499.9) was not significant under the primary exact test ($p{=}0.125$), the same small-sample disagreement pattern discussed in Section~\ref{ssec:disagreement}.

\subsection{The adaptive controller}
$\lambda_t = \lambda_{\text{base}} + \lambda_{\text{rep}}\max(0,\overline{\text{rep}}-\text{rep}_{\text{target}}) - \lambda_{\text{ent}}\max(0,\overline{H}-H_{\text{target}})$, clipped to $[0,\lambda_{\max}]$; the lower clip is a correctness requirement (an unclipped negative multiplier would reward risk).

\subsection{An FSD-style contrastive baseline}
I add a history-aware baseline in the spirit of the FSD family (Section~\ref{sec:related}): $\text{naive}(v)$ is the empirical next-token distribution implied purely by exact local repetition, and the decoder samples from $\mathrm{softmax}((\text{model\_logits} - \alpha\cdot\text{naive})/T)$. This differs from recurrence risk in using a \emph{continuous, frequency-weighted} contrast rather than a bounded projection with a distortion budget, and from repetition penalty in only suppressing candidates the naive local predictor is specifically confident about. It plugs into the same generic decoder interface (\texttt{step}/\texttt{reset}/\texttt{prime}) as recurrence risk; I verified it runs end-to-end without any change to the shared generation loop.

\subsection{An LZ Penalty baseline}
I additionally implement the real, published LZ Penalty \citep{ginart2025lzpenalty}. For a recent buffer $b$ (the last $B$ tokens) and an earlier lookback window $w$ (the $W$ tokens before that), let $(l,d)$ be the length and distance of the longest prefix-match of $b$ against $w$. For each candidate $a$, the codelength contribution is
\[
  \Delta|C_{\text{LZ}}|(a) = \begin{cases}
    \log_2 V & \text{no match at all}, \\
    \log_2 \delta & \text{a new, singleton match at distance } \delta, \\
    \log_2\!\left(1 - \frac{d-l+1}{ld}\right) - 1 & \text{extends the current match to length } l{+}1,
  \end{cases}
\]
applied as $\text{logits} \mathrel{+}= \alpha \cdot \Delta|C_{\text{LZ}}|$ -- novel candidates are boosted, candidates completing a long, close repeat are strongly suppressed, matching the paper's reported $[-5,+17]$-bit dynamic range for a subword configuration. I verified this reproduction against the paper's own qualitative description on a constructed case (a token completing a close 8-token repeat scores strongly negative; a token absent from the whole window scores exactly $\log_2 V$, the top of the range) and documented, in the implementation itself, one indexing-convention choice I could not fully verify against a reference implementation (Section~\ref{sec:related}). Buffer/window sizes are scaled down from the paper's subword defaults (32/512) for this project's much smaller character-level vocabulary.

\subsection{No-repeat blocking is excluded from ranking, not merely asterisked}
\label{ssec:hard-repeat}
A global no-repeat-$n$-gram filter, checked against the entire prior history, structurally cannot allow a persistent cycle of any period up to the generation length to form, since any such cycle reproduces a blocked $n$-gram at lag $P\le n$. Its 0/150 result is guaranteed by construction, not discovered by experiment, and I exclude it from every significance test and from the survival ranking.

This directly corroborates, with independent human judgment rather than only mcNLL, the qualitative claim already made descriptively in Section~\ref{ssec:qualitative}: \textsc{dual}'s exact per-step guarantee is purchased at a KL cost (11.2--12.7 bits) that produces text human readers consistently find less natural than any of the three heuristic methods, which reach the same 0/150 loop outcome at a fraction of the distortion and with no formal guarantee at all. \textbf{This is exactly the tension this paper's reframing (Section~\ref{ssec:discussion}) is about}: the dual mechanism is the only one whose per-step risk-bound is backed by a proof -- its 0/150 outcome, like every other method's here, is an observed count, not a proven zero probability (Section~\ref{sec:theory}) -- and it is also the one human readers like least among the methods that all reach that same observed outcome. Among the heuristics, FSD-style is the clear preferred method (93\% pooled win rate, beating both other zero-loop competitors), despite having the highest automatic spelling-error rate (Section~\ref{ssec:results-fsd-lz}) -- a reminder that mcNLL and spelling-error rate are not validated proxies for the human judgment actually collected here, and should not have been assumed to predict it.

I do not report this as a general claim about recurrence-risk decoding's human-perceived quality beyond this specific $T{=}0.10$, primary-model, 9-participant pilot; a properly powered study with a larger, more systematically recruited participant pool is a natural next step, not undertaken here.

\section{Toward Finite-Horizon Hazard: Synthetic Validation}
\label{sec:synthetic}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Construction}
A 20-state Markov chain: most states form a diffuse "safe" region, from which a small probability (0.05) leads to a \emph{gateway} state -- not itself an immediate repeat, but transitioning with high probability (0.75) into a tight 2-cycle. True hazard is computed by exact backward DP over (state, bounded 2-state trailing window, steps-remaining): $f[r][(s,\text{win})] = \sum_{s'} P[s,s']\big(\mathbb{1}[s'\in\text{win}] + \mathbb{1}[s'\notin\text{win}]\,f[r{-}1][(s',\text{win}')]\big)$, $f[0][\cdot]{=}0$. Per-candidate hazard $\haz(s')$ mirrors the real-model transfer's structure exactly: the probability of looping within the remaining horizon \emph{given that $s'$ is chosen now}.

\subsection{An earlier unreproducible claim, corrected}
An earlier version of this paper reported "17.9\% vs.\ 53.2\% loop rate at matched KL$\approx$0.24" as the headline synthetic result. I could not reproduce this number from its own saved artifact: the script's saved \texttt{matched\_\allowbreak{}distortion\_\allowbreak{}check} targeted a \emph{different} operating point than the one actually cited in the text, because the cited number came from a separate, ad hoc calculation I never integrated back into the script. I rewrote the script so the matched-distortion comparison is computed and saved for \textbf{every} point in the $\varepsilon$ grid at once, removing any possibility of silently reporting a hand-picked point, and re-ran it end to end.

This is the same boundary phenomenon as $\varepsilon=\min_v\risk(v)$ in the real dual solver (Section~\ref{sec:theory}): once $q$ is fully concentrated on the one-step risk-minimizing action(s), tightening $\varepsilon$ further cannot change $q$, so it cannot increase KL further either. Hazard's KL at $\varepsilon\in\{0.05,0.02,0.01\}$ (0.632, 0.810, 0.877) all exceed this $\approx0.613$-bit ceiling, so no local configuration, however aggressively tuned, can match hazard's distortion at those three settings -- these are marked \textbf{not comparable} in the table above and \textbf{excluded from the dominance count}, not credited as either a win or a loss for either mechanism. Among the three points where a matched-KL comparison is actually possible ($\varepsilon\ge0.10$), \textbf{hazard-aware control wins all three}, by margins of 26--36 percentage points. This is a narrower, more precisely scoped claim than "dominates at every matched-KL point" -- and a more interesting one: it locates a genuine structural gap between what local, one-step-only risk control can ever purchase with distortion budget and what a mechanism that reasons about the actual future loop event can, which is precisely the limitation this paper's synthetic experiment exists to characterize (see also Section~\ref{ssec:discussion}'s reframing of this paper's contribution around exactly this gap).

\subsection{The sequence-level oracle: exactly how inefficient is local control?}
\label{ssec:oracle}
"Local" and "hazard" above are both, in the end, ONE-STEP projections: each re-solves independently at every step from whatever risk signal it uses (an indicator for local, the exact multi-step hazard for hazard-aware), and neither ever accounts for the KL cost of the choices it will face LATER. Per review, I compute the true sequence-level optimum directly: the exact path measure $Q^*$ minimizing total path $\KL(Q\Vert P)$ subject to $Q(\text{loop within } H)\le\varepsilon$, via the standard exponential-tilting (Doob-transform) solution to a KL-constrained hitting-time problem -- this construction itself is not novel; the point is to measure exactly what local, one-step-only control gives up relative to it. Concretely: $g[r][(s,\text{win});\lambda] := \E_P[2^{-\lambda\cdot\mathbb{1}(\text{loop within }r)}\mid s,\text{win}]$ satisfies the SAME backward recursion as $f$ above, with the "loop happens now" branch weighted by $2^{-\lambda}$ instead of probability mass $1$; the optimal tilted kernel is $Q^*(s'\mid s,\text{win},r) \propto P(s,s')\cdot m(s',\text{win}',r{-}1)$ (the same per-branch term defining $g$), and $\lambda$ is bisected -- exactly mirroring this paper's own per-step dual solver (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), now applied over the whole horizon instead of one step -- until $Q^*$'s EXACTLY forward-computed (not simulated) loop probability matches each target $\varepsilon$.

Local and hazard's KL/oracle ratios move in OPPOSITE directions across the grid (local's ratio shrinks as $\varepsilon$ tightens, hazard's grows) -- consistent with the two mechanisms failing for different reasons: local because it cannot see the future risk at all, hazard because seeing it exactly is still not the same as pricing it into a path-level plan. \textbf{This is the concrete, quantified version of the limitation this paper's reframing (Section~\ref{ssec:discussion}) is about}: recurrence-risk decoding, exact per-step dual calibration included, is a LOCAL mechanism, and this table measures precisely how much that costs relative to the sequence-level control it is not.

\section{Attempted Transfer to the Real Model}
\label{sec:hazard-transfer}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Design}
I collected per-candidate hazard labels by forcing each of several candidate next-tokens (model's top-8 by probability, plus 4 lower-probability tokens) as the immediate next token, then continuing $K$ independent stochastic rollouts at $T{=}0.10$ for a fixed horizon, checking the frozen loop event. I diagnosed and fixed two structural problems before any interpretable data could be collected: a 20-character horizon that could not observe the event under any circumstance, and uniformly-random context selection that put most probed contexts nowhere near an actual loop.

\subsection{No detectable signal under this estimator}
Per-candidate hazard for "risky" ($\risk(v)>0$) vs.\ "novel" ($\risk(v)=0$) candidates showed no separation (0.027 vs.\ 0.028 mean hazard, $n{=}156$/$1644$). A multi-step-commitment variant also showed no significant separation at any tested commitment length $L\in\{1,3,5,8,15\}$, even before correction for testing five values (largest raw gap $p{=}0.137$ at $L{=}8$; Bonferroni-corrected $p{=}0.685$).

\textbf{My conclusion from these two experiments is deliberately narrow, per explicit review guidance}: under the specific rollout-based estimator, candidate-selection scheme, and per-cell sample sizes (5 rollouts per candidate) I used here, \textbf{I found no detectable hazard signal} distinguishing risky from novel candidates, at either single-token or the tested multi-step-commitment granularities. This is \emph{not} a claim that finite-horizon hazard control does not work on real language models, that the mechanism validated in Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} is invalid, or that no estimator could find a signal here; it is a statement about what this specific, small, non-context-clustered estimator did and did not detect. A properly powered, context-clustered re-estimation, with substantially more rollouts per candidate, is a natural next step I have not yet taken.

\subsection{What transfers and what does not}
The result on this second corpus is not a uniform "it also works" and I do not report it as one. Three findings, each load-bearing for a different part of the paper's argument:

\textbf{The per-step feasibility rate is consistent across both models; zero persistent loops is an empirical observation, not a proven consequence.} Under the final, corrected solver, \textsc{rr-dual} reaches \textbf{exactly 100.0\% per-step feasibility} on this corpus at both tested $\varepsilon$, identical to the primary model's 100.0\% (Table~\ref{tab:main}) -- a like-for-like comparison at last, both numbers now from the same, fully log-space, expanding-bracket solver. Feasibility rate is the quantity the theorem in Section~\ref{sec:theory} actually governs: it bounds the per-step \emph{expected} recurrence risk when the numerical projection succeeds, and that success rate is what stays consistent across corpora, because dual calibration re-solves for $\lambda$ from $p$ and $\risk$ at every single step rather than depending on any corpus-tuned constant. The theorem says nothing about the SEQUENCE-LEVEL count of persistent loops, so the fact that both models independently show 0/150 is reported here as exactly that -- a repeated empirical observation across two corpora -- and not described as something the theory predicts or as a guarantee that has "transferred."

\textbf{The fixed-hyperparameter modes do not.} \textsc{rr-fixed} ($\lambda{=}2$, unchanged from the primary model) rises from 2.7\% loop on the primary model to \textbf{51.3\%} here; \textsc{rr-adaptive} (unchanged targets) rises from 0.0\% to \textbf{21.3\%}. The gap between \textsc{rr-dual} and \textsc{rr-fixed} on this corpus is large and highly significant (paired exact test, $\Delta$RMST $=+60.8$, $p{=}0.0001$). This is a genuine, informative limitation, not a footnote: a fixed penalty strength implicitly suited to one corpus's risk and entropy statistics does not transfer its effectiveness to a corpus with different repetition statistics, even though the underlying mechanism (the same code, same hyperparameters) is identical. Qualitatively, \textsc{rr-fixed}'s output on this corpus is still visibly cyclical (\emph{``the same manner as the produce of the country, the country which they have been sufficient to the produce of the same manner, and the profits of the same manner as the produce of the produce of the land and labour of the produce of the produce of''}), confirming the numeric finding is not a measurement artifact.

\textbf{The primary model's temperature-safe regime does not transfer either.} $T{=}0.15$, which reached 0.0\% loop on the primary model, still shows \textbf{26.0\%} loop on this corpus. I did not re-run the temperature sweep (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) for this model, so I do not know where its safe threshold actually is, only that it is not at 0.15; this is reported as an open question, not resolved by assumption.

Repetition penalty and \textsc{rr-adaptive} both weaken substantially on this corpus (36.0\% and 21.3\% loop, up from 4.7\% and 0.0\%); SuffixMatch, FSD, LZ Penalty, and hard no-repeat all continue to reach 0/150, consistent with these being exact-match or hard-constraint mechanisms that do not depend on a corpus-tuned penalty magnitude the way \textsc{rr-fixed} and repetition penalty do. LZ Penalty's own effectiveness does, however, fail to transfer to GPT-2 (Section~\ref{sec:gpt2-full}, 0\% to 25\%) -- a reminder that "does not depend on a corpus-tuned magnitude" is itself corpus-and-model-specific, not a property guaranteed by the mechanism's design.

\subsection{A compact validation across three modern pretrained model families}
\label{ssec:modern-llm}
Per explicit review guidance that this check need not be exhaustive -- ``compact...does not need to be huge'' -- I extended the generalization question one step further: does any of this transfer to genuinely modern (2024--2025), instruction-capable-scale pretrained models, across more than one model family? I selected three checkpoints spanning roughly an order of magnitude in size and three different pretraining organizations: OLMo-2-0425-1B (Allen AI, 1B, fully open training data and recipe), Qwen2.5-3B (Alibaba, 3B), and Mistral-7B-v0.3 (Mistral AI, 7B). Every model follows the same pilot~$\to$~calibrate~$\to$~compare protocol already used for GPT-2 (Section~\ref{sec:gpt2-full}), and every full comparison that qualifies runs at the same reduced, compact scale across all three models: 5 seeds $\times$ 10 fresh prompts $\times$ 150 new tokens (50 samples per configuration) -- deliberately smaller than GPT-2's 10~seeds/200~tokens, and, once one model's scale was reduced for this reason, applied \emph{identically} to all three rather than left inconsistent across models, for the same reason floating-point precision was kept identical across all three below: this project has repeatedly found that an unexamined cross-model inconsistency, not any single number, is what turns into a defect.

\textbf{All three models ran in float32}, matching the primary and GPT-2 experiments, not the faster bfloat16 default this project's own code uses on CUDA -- chosen deliberately over a real speed/memory advantage, given this project's origin in a float32-vs-numerical-precision defect (Section~\ref{ssec:dual-fix}) that a reviewer would be right to ask about if precision varied across the very models being compared for consistency. Mistral-7B-v0.3's full weights do not fit in this project's 36GB unified-memory hardware without triggering swap under the default HuggingFace loading path (which briefly holds a full CPU copy and a full target-device copy simultaneously); weights were instead loaded directly onto the target device shard-by-shard (\texttt{device\_\allowbreak{}map} with \texttt{low\_\allowbreak{}cpu\_\allowbreak{}mem\_\allowbreak{}usage=True}), verified to produce bit-identical logits to the default loading path on a smaller model before being trusted for this comparison -- a memory-management fix, not a numerical one.

\textbf{The adaptive controller's benefit is itself model-dependent, not a property of the mechanism alone.} On OLMo, \textsc{rr-adaptive} cuts loop rate from the 12.0\% shared by both baselines and \textsc{rr-fixed} down to 2.0\%, at negligible KL cost (0.027 bits) -- confirmed not to be an artifact of the calibrated threshold by a full $(R, P_{\max})$ sensitivity sweep (5 values of $R\in\{4,...,8\}$ $\times$ 5 values of $P_{\max}\in\{10,...,40\}$, 25 grid points, using the raw generated token ids saved specifically for this check): the ordering $\textsc{rr-adaptive} \le \textsc{rr-fixed} \le$ baseline holds at \textbf{every single grid point, 0 reversals out of 25}. On Mistral, by contrast, \textsc{rr-adaptive} and \textsc{rr-fixed} are statistically indistinguishable from the $T{=}0.10$ baseline at the frozen threshold (4.0\% for all three), and the same sensitivity sweep shows no consistent direction at all: at looser thresholds ($R{=}3,4$) both RR variants sit \emph{below} baseline, at the calibrated and stricter thresholds ($R{=}5$--$7$, tight $P_{\max}$) they sit at or slightly \emph{above} it (e.g.\ $R{=}5,P_{\max}{=}10$: baseline 0.0\%, both RR variants 4.0\%). I do not have an explanation for why the same mechanism, same hyperparameters, and a comparable pretraining-data-era model helps substantially on one 2024-generation checkpoint and does nothing distinguishable from noise on another -- it is reported as exactly that, an unexplained model-dependence, not smoothed into either an "it works" or "it doesn't work" summary.

\textbf{The cheapest route to zero measured loops is not the same method on every model.} On OLMo, SuffixMatch is cheapest among the zero-loop group (0.223 bits) -- consistent with the same finding on GPT-2 (Section~\ref{ssec:common-kl}) and the primary model (Table~\ref{tab:commonkl}). On Mistral, SuffixMatch does not reach zero loops at all here (4.0\%); FSD-style is the cheapest that does (1.691 bits). A full $(R, P_{\max})$ sweep on the same seven headline configurations further shows this FSD result is not uniformly robust the way the OLMo and primary-model zero-loop findings are: at the loosest tested threshold ($R{=}3$) FSD shows 12.0\% loop on Mistral, dropping to a clean 0.0\% across the entire rest of the grid ($R\ge4$, all $P_{\max}$) -- the calibrated $R{=}5$ sits safely inside the robust region, but not by a wide margin. SuffixMatch, \textsc{rr-dual} (both $\varepsilon$), and every zero-loop finding on OLMo, by contrast, hold at exactly 0.0\% across the entire 25-point grid on both models, with one negligible exception (LZ Penalty on the primary character model's own grid, Section~\ref{ssec:naive-fails}-style check, shows 1.3\% at the single loosest cell only). \textsc{rr-dual} remains the most expensive route to zero loops on both models (4.39--4.59 bits on OLMo, 6.74--7.26 bits on Mistral), consistent with every other model tested in this paper.

\textbf{Generation-length censoring is not hiding a materially different picture at 150 tokens.} Re-censoring each already-generated sample's saved onset position at shorter horizons (30, 60, 90, 120 tokens) rather than only the final 150 shows the OLMo \textsc{rr-adaptive} advantage opening up specifically in the back half of generation (0.0\% for both \textsc{rr-adaptive} and baseline through 90 tokens; baseline reaches 10--12\% by 150 tokens while \textsc{rr-adaptive} reaches only 0--2\%), not a horizon-specific artifact; the Mistral null finding holds at every horizon tested, not only 150.

\textsc{rr-dual} reaches \textbf{exactly 100.0\% per-step feasibility on both OLMo and Mistral} (both $\varepsilon$, zero structurally-infeasible and zero near-boundary steps), extending the guarantee's unbroken record to five models across three tokenizer/architecture families (the primary character model, its independently-trained second checkpoint, GPT-2, OLMo, Mistral) without a single exception -- the one number in this whole compact validation that is not model-dependent.

Taken together with the fully-robust GPT-2 character-level $(R, P_{\max})$ sensitivity check (25 grid points, all zero-loop findings unchanged), this compact validation is the clearest illustration in this paper that a mechanism's effectiveness -- as opposed to the dual mechanism's per-step feasibility guarantee, which does not depend on any corpus- or model-tuned constant and has now been checked on five models across three tokenizer/architecture families without exception -- is an empirical property of a specific model, not a property that can be assumed to transfer from one modern pretrained checkpoint to the next merely because both are ``modern'' and of comparable scale.

%==============================================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{ssec:discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{What is supported}
Restricted to the validated, reproducible event: \textsc{rr-dual}, once its multiplier cap is raised enough to be genuinely feasible, eliminates persistent loops on both the primary and the second, independently trained model, at an honestly measured and corpus-consistent cost ($\sim$10--12 bits at $\varepsilon{=}0.05$ on both models). This transfer is the strongest generalization evidence in this paper, precisely because it follows from the theory (exact per-step recalibration) rather than from any corpus-specific tuning. The compact three-model-family modern-LLM validation (Section~\ref{ssec:modern-llm}) extends this specific claim, and only this claim, further: 100.0\% per-step feasibility on OLMo-2-0425-1B and Mistral-7B-v0.3 too, five models and three tokenizer/architecture families without a single exception. \textsc{rr-fixed} reduces loop rate substantially on the primary model at very low cost, but that effectiveness -- unlike dual's -- does not transfer to the second corpus without re-tuning. On the synthetic problem, hazard-aware control dominates a local-only proxy at every distortion budget where the comparison is meaningful (3/3 matched-KL points); at the remaining three tested budgets, local's own structural KL ceiling ($\approx0.613$ bits/step, Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}) makes the comparison inapplicable rather than a further win, which is itself the more informative finding -- a concrete, quantified limit on what local, one-step-only control can ever purchase with distortion budget, regardless of how it is tuned.

\subsection{What is not supported}
Recurrence-risk decoding is not shown to improve survival over baselines at ordinary sampling temperatures, where the primary model does not exhibit the targeted pathology. \textsc{rr-fixed} is not shown to beat repetition penalty on survival alone on the primary model ($p{=}0.246$). The hazard-aware mechanism is not shown to transfer to the real model at the granularities I tested (Section~\ref{sec:hazard-transfer}), and I do not generalize this null result beyond that specific estimator. On a pretrained subword model (GPT-2, Section~\ref{sec:gpt2-full}), the dual guarantee reaches the same 0/100-loop, 100\%-feasible outcome as on the primary model, at roughly half the KL cost; every other configuration's effectiveness shifts by a different amount and in a different direction than it did on the non-fiction corpus, most sharply for the LZ Penalty baseline (0\% on both the primary model and the non-fiction corpus, but 25\% on GPT-2) -- I read this as further evidence that only the dual mechanism's guarantee, not any specific configuration's effectiveness, is the thing that generalizes. The human evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:human-eval}, $n{=}9$) is a small, informally-recruited pilot; it establishes a clear preference ranking among the zero-loop group at this one temperature, on this one primary model, and should not be read as a general claim about recurrence-risk decoding's human-perceived quality beyond that scope. The common-distortion (common-KL) comparison (Section~\ref{ssec:common-kl}, Table~\ref{tab:commonkl}) is a single-checkpoint, single-temperature ($T{=}0.10$) snapshot; whether the same cross-family ranking (SuffixMatch/LZ Penalty cheap, FSD and no-repeat surprisingly costly, dual most costly by construction) holds at other operating points is untested. The modern-LLM validation (Section~\ref{ssec:modern-llm}) sharpens this into a positive finding of its own, not only a caveat: the adaptive controller's substantial benefit on OLMo (12.0\%$\to$2.0\% loop, confirmed by a 25-point detector-threshold sweep with zero reversals) is indistinguishable from noise on Mistral under the identical sweep, and the cheapest zero-loop baseline differs by model (SuffixMatch on OLMo and GPT-2, FSD-style -- itself only conditionally robust there -- on Mistral). None of the heuristic mechanisms' effectiveness is shown to be a property of "modern, billion-scale pretrained model" as a category; each result is model-specific, and Qwen2.5-3B shows no measurable instance of the targeted pathology at all at the compute budget tested, which is reported as a null result bounded by that budget, not as evidence the mechanism or the failure mode is scale-immune.

\subsection{Wording discipline}
Dual calibration guarantees a one-step bound on \emph{expected} recurrence risk at each decoding step, given the numerical projection succeeds (Section~\ref{ssec:dual-fix}: feasibility is now checked in closed form against $\min_v\risk(v)$, with the search bracket expanding as needed rather than capped); it does not bound the sequence-level probability of ever looping, and every 0/150 figure in this paper is reported as exactly that -- an observed count, not a proven zero probability.

\textbf{Two numerically load-bearing defects were found, fixed, and confirmed by a full rerun.} (1) The dual solver materialized $p$ via a plain softmax and computed $\log(p+10^{-40})$, which at $T{=}0.10$ silently floors many underflowed (but truly distinct) low-probability tokens to an identical, incorrect log-probability -- reproduced directly on this project's own model (276/2000 vocabulary entries underflowed in one constructed check, with true log-probabilities spanning 41 nats that the old code collapsed to a single floor value). (2) The model-consistency NLL's batching left-padded variable-length context windows with a real vocabulary token id, which this architecture's unmasked causal attention then legitimately attended to, corrupting the scored position's logits (verified: 0.26 max logit difference between an isolated and a padded evaluation of the identical context on a toy configuration). Both are fixed (log-space solver with an expanding bracket; right-padding, which causal masking provably cannot let leak into real positions), verified numerically in isolation, and confirmed on the full rerun reported in Table~\ref{tab:main}: dual feasibility on the corrected code is \textbf{exactly 100.0\%} at both tested $\varepsilon$, with zero mean and zero maximum violation, up from an original 72.3--74.5\% (the diagnostic-visibility defect) and an intermediate 98.75--98.87\% (the multiplier-cap defect, still affected by the float32 bug). Every table in this paper reflects the fully corrected code and the \textsc{cosine} checkpoint used throughout.

\subsection{Limitations}
The loop event's $\sim$1.3\% pooled false-positive rate on the primary battery hides real per-corpus variance, not just sampling noise around a uniform rate: \textbf{Stoker's \emph{Dracula} alone fires at 26/300 = 8.7\% (Table~\ref{tab:validation}), roughly 7$\times$ the pooled figure and outside every other corpus's CI}, concentrated in epistolary formulas (repeated salutations, dated-entry openings) that this syntactic event cannot distinguish from genuine degeneration. Quoting "$\sim$1.3\%" alone, without this breakdown, would overstate how uniformly reliable the event is on prose that shares Dracula's formulaic structure; every loop-rate number in this paper should be read with that caveat attached, not just the pooled validation figure. The second corpus's $\sim$0.8\% is a pooled figure over its own, different battery (Section~\ref{sec:generalization}) and has not been checked for a similar single-corpus concentration. The event is a syntactic definition regardless; semantic repetition is not captured. mcNLL remains an in-distribution proxy, not a validated human-quality measure, independent of the padding-corruption fix above. The residual dual infeasibility is now exactly $0.0\%$ on both the \textsc{cosine} and \textsc{baseline} checkpoints under the corrected solver (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), so the earlier question of whether a nonzero residual reflected a genuine structural floor or an insufficiently large cap no longer arises on this data; it could still arise on a different model or risk configuration, and the closed-form check (Section~\ref{ssec:dual-fix}) is what would resolve it if it did. The FSD-style baseline is a good-faith reimplementation, not a certified reproduction; the LZ Penalty baseline follows a real, verified, unambiguous published formula, but is not itself a certified reproduction either -- it changes one indexing convention (Section~\ref{sec:theory}) relative to the published algorithm, since no reference implementation was available to verify the intended reading against, and should not be called "authentic." The renamed \textsc{SuffixMatchDecoder} (formerly, misleadingly, \textsc{LookBackDecoder}) has never claimed to be the published Look-back algorithm and should continue not to be read as one. The non-fiction generalization result (Table~\ref{tab:generalization}) has now been rerun in full under the corrected code; an earlier version of this table was also still using the pre-rename "Look-back" label for \textsc{SuffixMatchDecoder} and omitted the LZ Penalty baseline and $\varepsilon{=}0.01$ entirely, both corrected here. The second-corpus generalization still did not re-run the temperature sweep, repetition-penalty sweep, or fixed-$\lambda$ re-tuning that might restore \textsc{rr-fixed}'s effectiveness there. The GPT-2 full comparison (Section~\ref{sec:gpt2-full}) uses only $T{=}0.10$ and did not re-run the temperature sweep, repetition-penalty sweep, or a re-tuned $\lambda$/$\alpha$ that might narrow \textsc{rr-fixed}/\textsc{rr-adaptive}'s gap to the primary model there, mirroring the same open question already noted above for the non-fiction corpus. Its risk order ($n_{\min}{=}2,n_{\max}{=}4$ tokens) and LZ Penalty buffer/window (32/512) were set once, from the paper's own reported subword defaults and this project's existing subword-decoder precedent (\texttt{generalize\_\allowbreak{}gpt2.py}), not swept. The human evaluation's 9 participants were recruited informally rather than through a formal, demographically-controlled panel, and all sessions used the primary character model at a single temperature ($T{=}0.10$); whether the same preference ranking holds at other temperatures, on the non-fiction corpus, or on GPT-2 is untested. The modern-LLM validation (Section~\ref{ssec:modern-llm}) is compact by explicit review allowance (5 seeds $\times$ 10 prompts $\times$ 150 tokens per configuration, half the sample count used for the primary and GPT-2 comparisons) and covers only $T{=}0.10$; wider CIs are the direct consequence and are reported, not hidden. Neither modern-LLM calibration met the pre-declared 1\% tolerance (OLMo: 1.38\% worst-case upper CI; Mistral: 3.15\%), the same honestly-reported fallback already used for GPT-2's event. Only two of the three selected model families (OLMo, Mistral) yielded a measurable regime and a full comparison; Qwen2.5-3B did not, at the temperatures and token budget tested, and an attempt to probe lower temperatures was abandoned incomplete for a memory-management reason unrelated to the scientific question, not reported with any number attached. The FSD-style zero-loop result on Mistral is the one zero-loop finding in this entire paper shown by a threshold-sensitivity sweep to be conditionally robust rather than unconditionally so (0.0\% for $R\ge4$, 12.0\% at $R{=}3$); the calibrated $R{=}5$ sits inside the robust region, but this is disclosed rather than smoothed over. Detector-threshold sensitivity for the modern-LLM comparisons was checked only for a seven-configuration subset (the recurrence-risk family plus SuffixMatch and FSD-style), not the full fourteen, to keep the added compute proportionate; generation-length sensitivity was checked for all fourteen, since it required no regeneration.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================

Recurrence-risk decoding, precisely specified as a minimum-distortion projection, removes a real, independently validated failure mode at an exactly quantified cost. The theorem behind it guarantees a per-step bound on \emph{expected} recurrence risk when the numerical projection succeeds, and that success rate -- not the sequence-level loop count, which the theorem does not speak to -- is what I find consistent across an independently trained model on a disjoint corpus (100.0\% feasibility on both, Table~\ref{tab:generalization}, under the final corrected solver), while the cheaper heuristic variants that approximate the same projection do not carry over their effectiveness. Zero persistent loops was observed on both models; I report this as a replicated empirical finding, not as a predicted or guaranteed consequence of the theorem. Reaching this point required finding and fixing, across the revisions of this project: a mis-stated infinite-penalty limit (found wrong twice, not once -- the first correction was itself incomplete, Section~\ref{sec:theory}), an unvalidated loop event, a seed-nondeterminism bug that made a validation report unreproducible, a chunked NLL implementation that under-conditioned roughly half its scored positions, a diagnostic-discarding bug that hid a solver silently failing $\sim$27\% of the time, a float32-underflow bug in the dual solver's probability handling that materially changed $q$, $\lambda$, and KL at low temperature, a padding-corruption bug in the NLL computation, an unreproducible synthetic-experiment claim, a theorem that omitted the $\varepsilon=\min_v\risk(v)$ boundary entirely (where no finite dual variable exists, and where a float32/float64 comparison could separately misclassify a feasible point as infeasible), a "no ceiling" description of a search that is in fact capped at 60 doublings, an "authentic" label on a reimplementation with a documented, unverified indexing deviation, a synthetic hazard-dominance claim that did not distinguish a genuine matched-KL comparison from a local-control ceiling neither grid resolution nor further tuning can cross, a pooled detector false-positive rate that read as uniform while concentrating 7$\times$ above the pooled figure on one corpus, and a NaN silently entering the KL/entropy diagnostics whenever top-$p$ filtering ran after the projection with $q(v){=}0$ exactly. Each defect changed a number I report here, in one case by a factor of five. The extension to finite-horizon hazard control was validated where ground truth is exactly known and found not to transfer to the real model under the specific estimator I tested -- reported as a scope-limited null result, not a general failure. Measured exactly against the sequence-level oracle (Section~\ref{ssec:oracle}), even that hazard-aware mechanism -- a one-step projection using the TRUE multi-step hazard as its risk signal -- spends on average 2.8$\times$ the KL a policy that properly prices in future distortion would need; local spends 2.7$\times$. This is the clearest evidence in this paper for the reframing it argues for throughout: recurrence-risk decoding, dual calibration included, is fundamentally a LOCAL mechanism, and the gap to sequence-level control is not a rounding error. The human evaluation, administered to 9 participants, is the one piece of this project that most directly ties the theory back to what a reader actually experiences: \textsc{dual}'s guarantee is real, but so is its cost, and independent human judgment now confirms that cost is perceptible, not merely a number in a KL column. The common-distortion comparison (Section~\ref{ssec:common-kl}) closes the reviewer's requested scope for this round: scored against one shared reference and one shared risk definition, the cheapest route to zero measured loops overall is \textsc{rr-adaptive} (0.096 bits, unguaranteed); among the four external baselines, SuffixMatch is cheapest, repetition penalty is comparably cheap in KL despite NOT reaching zero loops at this operating point and being the slowest method tested, FSD-style and the hard no-repeat constraint are markedly more expensive than their simplicity suggests among the methods that do reach zero, and \textsc{rr-dual} is the most expensive by construction -- exactly the kind of cross-family comparison no single decoder's own bookkeeping could have produced. The compact validation across three modern pretrained model families (Section~\ref{ssec:modern-llm}) is the final and, taken together with the sequence-level oracle (Section~\ref{ssec:oracle}), most direct evidence for this paper's reframing: \textsc{rr-dual}'s per-step feasibility guarantee reaches exactly 100.0\% on OLMo-2-0425-1B and Mistral-7B-v0.3 without exception, the fifth and sixth models across three tokenizer/architecture families on which it has now held -- while every heuristic's effectiveness, including the adaptive controller's substantial and threshold-sensitivity-confirmed benefit on OLMo, is shown to be a property of a specific model rather than of "modern pretrained language model" as a category: the identical mechanism does nothing distinguishable from noise on Mistral, and Qwen2.5-3B shows no instance of the targeted pathology at all at the budget tested. Local, one-step recurrence-risk control -- proven, calibrated, and now checked across five models -- removes a real and precisely quantified failure mode exactly where its guarantee says it will, and its heuristic relatives remove it only where an unexplained, model-specific alignment happens to hold; closing that gap is a question for sequence-level control, which this paper's oracle and hazard-transfer results (Sections~\ref{ssec:oracle}, \ref{sec:hazard-transfer}) show is not yet answered by any one-step mechanism tested here, guaranteed or not.

\section{Order-of-Magnitude Estimate for the Multiplier Cap}
\label{app:lambda-estimate}
For a two-outcome simplification (one risky token with $\risk{=}1$, one safe token with $\risk{=}0$, raw pre-temperature probability ratio $\rho$ risky:safe), the $\lambda$ needed to bring $q(\text{risky})\le\varepsilon$ at temperature $T$ is $\lambda \ge \ln\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon} + \frac{1}{T}\ln\rho$. At $T{=}0.10$: $\rho{=}100$ needs $\lambda\approx50$; $\rho{=}1000$ needs $\lambda\approx73$; a cap of 1000 comfortably covers any $\rho$ up to astronomically large values ($\ln\rho\lesssim97$), which is why raising it resolved the vast majority of the observed infeasibility (Section~\ref{ssec:dual-fix}). This estimate is for an ordinary interior point, away from the $\varepsilon=\min_v\risk(v)$ boundary (Section~\ref{sec:theory}); at that exact boundary the $\lambda$ needed depends instead on how small a float64 gap the solver must resolve against a float32-rounded risk floor, not on any probability ratio, which is a different (and, per the \texttt{near\_\allowbreak{}boundary} tolerance fix, now separately handled) regime from the one this estimate covers. The 60-doubling cap ($\lambda$ up to $2^{60}\approx1.15\times10^{18}$) exceeds even the $\rho{=}1000$ estimate above by roughly 16 orders of magnitude, which is why it is not observed to bind in practice on this project's data (Section~\ref{ssec:dual-fix}) outside that boundary case.
