\begin{abstract}
Autoregressive language models sometimes fall into repetition loops. Decoders that suppress repetition act one token at a time on a local proxy for loop risk, whereas the quantity of interest is the probability that a loop occurs anywhere in the generated sequence. This paper asks why per-step repetition control can be inefficient for preventing a loop across the whole sequence, in two parts.

First, in a finite-state system small enough to solve exactly, we compute the minimum-KL policy for a given loop probability and compare it with per-step controllers that re-solve a one-step constraint at every token. When their risk signal is a repeat indicator they need 2.3--5.1 times the optimal KL (mean 3.1), and when it is the exact multi-step hazard they still need 1.6--3.3 times (mean 2.2). The optimum has a closed form that follows from the chain rule for KL applied to the loop event $E$: moving its probability from $p$ to $\epsilon$ costs at least $d_{\mathrm{Ber}}(\epsilon\Vert p)$, the Bernoulli KL, with equality for the reweighting of whole paths by whether they loop. A causal sequential rule realizes this reweighting when the exact event-to-go hazard is available, so the gap is not a statement about causality. Using the hazard in an exponential instead of the optimal affine form, with one multiplier for the whole generation, costs at most about 10\% more KL than the optimum in this system; re-solving a fixed budget at every step costs 1.6--3.3 times the optimum. The loss is in how distortion is allocated across steps.

Second, on a character-level language model we use recurrence-risk decoding, a KL projection whose dual-calibrated mode solves the per-step problem exactly, as an instrument for the same question. Prompt-conditional estimates from 150 generations per decoder put the part of each decoder's path KL that is needed to move the loop probability at 0.2--0.5 bits, against 19 to 6300 bits actually spent, so at least 98\% of the distortion the decoders spend is estimated to reshape the generation on each side of the loop event and not to move its probability; these are descriptive plug-in estimates with intervals from resampling prompts, and they do not say how much a practical decoder could save. We report observed loop counts and not guarantees, and we do not read KL as a measure of readability: a pilot human evaluation with nine participants found no reliable readability difference among four decoders. The paper does not propose recurrence-risk decoding as a practical decoder. When streaming is not required, rejecting generations that contain a loop attains the event-level bound exactly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================

A recurring failure mode of autoregressive text generation is the repetition loop: the model emits a short span and then reproduces it until the sample is dominated by a fixed cycle. It is most visible under low-temperature or deterministic decoding, but the underlying mechanism, the model assigning high probability to continuing a pattern it has already produced, is present at every scale. It is a decoding-time phenomenon: the same trained weights can yield fluent text or a degenerate loop depending only on how the next token is chosen.

Most remedies are decoding rules applied one token at a time. They penalize candidates that would repeat recent text, or contrast the model against a record of what it has already generated. Whether a loop occurs, however, is a property of the whole sequence. A rule that looks only at the next token can be tuned to reduce repetition, but it has no direct handle on the probability that a loop occurs later, and the distortion it introduces is spent at every step whether or not a loop is at stake. We ask: \emph{why can controlling repetition at each step be inefficient for preventing a loop across the whole sequence, and by how much?}

To make the question precise we compare three kinds of rule (Section~\ref{sec:formal}). A \emph{local surrogate controller} re-solves, at every step, a one-step constrained problem with a fixed per-step budget, using a one-step proxy for loop risk. \emph{Sequence-aware causal control} uses the probability that a loop occurs in the rest of the generation, the event-to-go hazard, with a single multiplier for the whole generation. The \emph{event-level optimum} is the sequence distribution closest to the model, in KL, among those that make a loop occur with a given probability. We show that the optimum is the reweighting of whole paths by whether they loop, that it costs exactly the Bernoulli KL $d_{\mathrm{Ber}}(\epsilon\Vert p)$, and that a causal sequential rule realizes it (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The mathematics is standard and is credited in Section~\ref{sec:related}; what we add is its use as a yardstick for repetition control.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{An exact comparison in a small system} (Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}). On a 20-state chain with horizon $H{=}6$ the optimum, the local controllers, and the loop probabilities are all computed exactly. Local surrogate controllers need 1.6--5.1 times the optimal KL at the same loop probability, even when their risk signal is the exact multi-step hazard. An ablation shows that the gap comes from re-solving a fixed budget at every step, not from the functional form in which the hazard enters. We verify the closed form against the sequential computation to $10^{-14}$ and compare local controllers at exactly equal distortion.
  \item \textbf{A qualified real-model analysis} (Section~\ref{sec:realmodel}). On a character-level language model with a validated loop detector we use recurrence-risk decoding, whose dual-calibrated mode solves the per-step problem exactly, together with five baseline decoders, to measure how much path KL each decoder spends relative to the event-level term. The comparison is conditioned on the prompt, and it is reported as plug-in estimates with intervals obtained by resampling whole prompts; it is not a bound with guaranteed coverage, and it does not say how much a practical decoder could save.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope}
Recurrence-risk decoding is used here as a tool for studying the question above, not proposed as the best practical decoder. We make no claim that it eliminates repetition loops in general: every result of zero observed loops is a count at one temperature, on a few models, and the per-step guarantee of the dual mode is a statement about the one-step problem, not about the probability of a loop. High KL is not treated as evidence of poor readability. Further models, the loop-detector validation, baseline details, a pilot human evaluation, a rollout-based attempt to estimate the hazard on a real model, and the implementation defects found along the way are in the appendices.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%==============================================================================

\textbf{Mathematical background.} The one-step problem of Section~\ref{sec:theory} is an I-projection of a distribution onto a linear family, whose solution is an exponential tilt of the reference \citep{csiszar1975idivergence}; its dual is the convex program of \citet{boyd2004convex}. The chain rule for relative entropy that underlies Section~\ref{ssec:bernoulli-bound} is standard \citep{cover2006elements}. The optimal event-level policy is a Doob $h$-transform of the reference process \citep{doob1957conditional}, and the sequential form of KL-regularized control, in which the optimal controlled transition reweights the uncontrolled one by an exponentiated value function, is the theory of linearly solvable control \citep{kappen2005linear,todorov2007linearly}. We do not claim any of these identities as new.

\textbf{Local token control and global distortion.} That constraining or reweighting a language model token by token induces a sequence distribution different from a sequence-level target is established outside the repetition-loop setting. Controlled decoding \citep{mudgal2024controlled} learns a value function to steer per-token choices toward a KL-regularized reward optimum; twisted Sequential Monte Carlo \citep{zhao2024twisted} and its extension to syntactic and semantic constraints \citep{loula2025syntactic} correct a token-level proposal with particle reweighting and resampling because the proposal alone does not sample from the target sequence distribution; and adaptive weighted rejection sampling \citep{lipkin2025awrs} makes constrained token-level sampling exact for a broad class of constraints without particles. This paper does not claim that the gap between local and global control is a new observation. Its contribution is narrower: for repetition-loop reliability specifically, it computes the size of that gap exactly in a small system, and estimates it, with stated caveats, on a real model.

\textbf{Decoding strategies for repetition.} Temperature sampling rescales logits by $1/T$ before the softmax. Nucleus sampling \citep{holtzman2020curious} restricts sampling to the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds $p$. Repetition penalty \citep{keskar2019ctrl} divides the logit of every previously generated token by a constant, a global discount that does not condition on whether a repeated $n$-gram is imminent. Typical sampling \citep{meister2023typical} keeps tokens whose surprisal is close to the distribution's entropy, and Mirostat \citep{basu2021mirostat} adjusts a truncation threshold online to hold surprisal near a target. No-repeat-$n$-gram blocking \citep{paulus2018deep} zeroes the probability of any token that would complete an already-seen $n$-gram. History-aware baselines used here are a longest-suffix-match heuristic (\textsc{SuffixMatch}, homemade and not attributed to any publication), an FSD-style decoder adapted from Frustratingly Simple Decoding \citep{yang2023frustratingly}, and a decoder that applies the penalty of the LZ Penalty \citep{ginart2025lzpenalty} with adapted history handling; their relation to the published methods is stated exactly in Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}.

\textbf{Survival analysis for degeneration.} A sample that never loops within the observation window is a right-censored observation, not an outlier. Loop onset is therefore treated as a survival time, estimated with Kaplan--Meier \citep{kaplan1958nonparametric} and summarized by restricted mean survival time (RMST). The character-level models are decoder-only Transformers \citep{vaswani2017attention}.

\section{Setup and Definitions}
\label{sec:formal}
%==============================================================================

Let $p(\cdot\mid x_{<t})$ denote the model's next-token distribution over the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ at step $t$, taken at the sampling temperature $T$, so $p=\mathrm{softmax}(\text{logits}/T)$. A \emph{decoder} defines a sampling distribution $q_t$ at each step. Given a prompt, $P$ denotes the distribution over whole generations $x_{1:N}$ induced by sampling from $p$ (the \emph{reference}, plain temperature sampling) and $Q$ the one induced by the decoder. \textbf{Every KL divergence in this paper is in bits.} By the chain rule, $\KL(Q\Vert P)=\E_Q\sum_{t}\KL(q_t\Vert p_t)$, so the total path divergence of a decoder is estimated by summing the per-step divergences along its own samples; a ``per-step mean'' divides that sum by the fixed generation length $N$. At $T{=}0.10$ the reference is already sharpened and low-entropy, so a KL against it measures movement within a distribution that leaves little room to move cheaply.

\textbf{The loop event.} A detector $\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{V}^{*}\to\{0,1\}$ defines the \emph{loop onset} $\tau=\min\{t\le N:\mathcal{L}(x_{1:t})=1\}$, with $\tau=\infty$ (right-censored) if no such $t$ exists. The event of interest is $E=\{\tau\le N\}$, a loop by the end of the generation. Appendix~\ref{sec:event} develops and validates the detector used for the character-level model and states its limitations.

\textbf{Recurrence risk.} For a candidate $v$ and $n$-gram orders $[n_{\min},n_{\max}]$,
\[
  \risk(v)=\frac{1}{N_o}\sum_{n=n_{\min}}^{n_{\max}}\mathbf{1}[(\text{last } n{-}1 \text{ tokens},v)\text{ has occurred earlier}],\quad N_o=n_{\max}-n_{\min}+1.
\]

\textbf{Three kinds of rule.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Local surrogate control.} At every step the controller solves $\min_q\KL(q\Vert p_t)$ subject to $\E_q[r_t]\le\varepsilon_{\text{step}}$, independently of every other step, for a one-step proxy $r_t(v)$ and a fixed per-step budget $\varepsilon_{\text{step}}$. The proxy is the recurrence risk $\risk(v)$ (a \emph{repeat indicator}), or, in the small system of Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}, the exact event-to-go hazard $H(h,v)$ defined in the next item. All decoders run on real models in this paper are of the first kind or are history-based heuristics that likewise use only information about the next token.
  \item \emph{Sequence-aware causal control.} At every step the rule uses the event-to-go hazard $H(h,v)=P(E\text{ in the rest of the generation}\mid h,v)$, where $h$ is the history, with a single multiplier fixed for the whole generation. It is causal and sequential. Section~\ref{ssec:bernoulli-bound} shows that its affine-hazard form realizes the event-level optimum.
  \item \emph{The event-level optimum} $Q^\star$ minimizes $\KL(Q\Vert P)$ subject to $Q(E)=\epsilon$. It is a benchmark, defined on whole sequences.
\end{itemize}
The dual mode of recurrence-risk decoding solves the local surrogate problem exactly at every step (Section~\ref{sec:theory}); this is a per-step guarantee and says nothing about $Q(E)$.

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{The one-step projection and dual calibration}
\begin{equation}
  \min_q \; \KL(q\Vert p) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \E_q[\risk]\le\varepsilon,\quad q\in\Delta(\mathcal{V}). \tag{P}
\end{equation}
The Lagrangian gives, for fixed $\lambda\ge0$, $q_\lambda(v)\propto p(v)\exp(-\lambda\risk(v))$ (F). By weak duality this solves (P) \emph{only} for the $\lambda$ that satisfies complementary slackness ($\lambda{=}0$ if $\E_p[\risk]\le\varepsilon$, else the unique $\lambda$ with $\E_{q_\lambda}[\risk]=\varepsilon$); any other $\lambda$ solves the different, unconstrained problem $\min_q\KL(q\Vert p)+\lambda\E_q[\risk]$ (R). Three modes follow: \textsc{dual} solves (P) exactly at every step, \textsc{fixed} solves (R) with a constant $\lambda$, and \textsc{adaptive} sets $\lambda$ by a heuristic with no guarantee.

If $\varepsilon\ge\E_p[\risk]$ the constraint is inactive and $q{=}p$. If $\varepsilon<\min_v\risk(v)$, (P) is infeasible. At exactly $\varepsilon=\min_v\risk(v)$, (P) is feasible but no finite $\lambda$ attains it through (F): $\E_{q_\lambda}[\risk]$ approaches $\min_v\risk(v)$ only as $\lambda\to\infty$, and in floating point the solver uses a tolerance and records a separate flag (Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}). As $\lambda\to\infty$ for a single $n$-gram order, (F) keeps exactly the $\risk{=}0$ tokens, as hard no-repeat-$n$ blocking does, but when no $\risk{=}0$ token exists hard blocking bans the whole vocabulary and the sampler fails, whereas (F) at a large finite $\lambda$ concentrates on the minimum-risk tokens.

\subsection{The event-level decomposition}
\label{ssec:bernoulli-bound}
Let $Q$ and $P$ be path measures on a common sequence space and $E$ any event, here $E=\{\tau\le N\}$. The chain rule for relative entropy applied to the binary variable $Z=\mathbf{1}[E]$ gives
\begin{multline*}
  \KL(Q\Vert P) \;=\; d_{\mathrm{Ber}}\bigl(Q(E)\,\Vert\,P(E)\bigr) \;+\; Q(E)\,\KL\bigl(Q(\cdot\mid E)\,\Vert\,P(\cdot\mid E)\bigr) \\
  \;+\; \bigl(1-Q(E)\bigr)\,\KL\bigl(Q(\cdot\mid E^c)\,\Vert\,P(\cdot\mid E^c)\bigr),
\end{multline*}
where $d_{\mathrm{Ber}}(a\Vert b)=a\log_2\frac{a}{b}+(1-a)\log_2\frac{1-a}{1-b}$. This is the identity $\KL(Q\Vert P)=\KL(Q_Z\Vert P_Z)+\E_{Z\sim Q}[\KL(Q_{X\mid Z}\Vert P_{X\mid Z})]$ for the coarse-graining $X\mapsto Z$; it needs no Markov structure and no assumption about decoding.

\begin{corollary}[Minimum KL to move an event probability]
\label{cor:min}
With $p=P(E)\in(0,1)$,
\[
  \min_{Q:\,Q(E)=\epsilon} \KL(Q\Vert P) \;=\; d_{\mathrm{Ber}}(\epsilon\Vert p),
\]
attained uniquely by $Q^\star(x)=P(x)\,\epsilon/p$ for $x\in E$ and $Q^\star(x)=P(x)\,(1-\epsilon)/(1-p)$ for $x\notin E$.
\end{corollary}
\noindent The last two terms of the decomposition are non-negative and each is minimized to zero by $Q(\cdot\mid E)=P(\cdot\mid E)$ and $Q(\cdot\mid E^c)=P(\cdot\mid E^c)$, which does not conflict with any value of $Q(E)$. The difference $\KL(Q\Vert P)-d_{\mathrm{Ber}}(Q(E)\Vert P(E))$ is therefore the distortion of the \emph{conditional shape} of the generation on each side of the event, the part of a decoder's divergence that is not needed to move the event probability.

\begin{corollary}[A causal sequential realization]
\label{cor:affine}
Let $2^{-\lambda}=\epsilon(1-p)/[p(1-\epsilon)]$, so that $Q^\star(x)\propto P(x)\,2^{-\lambda\mathbf{1}[x\in E]}$. For a history $h$ that has not yet triggered $E$,
\[ Q^\star(v\mid h)\;\propto\;P(v\mid h)\,\bigl[\,1-(1-2^{-\lambda})\,H(h,v)\,\bigr], \]
where $H(h,v)=P(E\mid h,v)$ is the probability that $E$ occurs in the rest of the generation given that $v$ is chosen from $h$, with $H(h,v){:=}1$ if $v$ completes a loop at once. The identity needs no Markov assumption; the Markov structure of the small system of Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} only makes $H$ computable exactly.
\end{corollary}
\noindent This is the Doob $h$-transform of $P$ with respect to $E$: $\E_P[2^{-\lambda\mathbf{1}[E]}\mid h,v]=1-(1-2^{-\lambda})H(h,v)$. It is causal and sequential, it uses one multiplier $\lambda$ for the whole generation, and it depends on the history only through the exact hazard $H$ for the remaining horizon. A causally committed, token-by-token rule can therefore realize the event-level optimum; what it needs is the event-to-go hazard, and what a local surrogate controller lacks is that hazard together with a way to share one distortion budget across steps.

\begin{corollary}[Rejection sampling at $\epsilon=0$]
\label{cor:reject}
Sampling a generation from $P$ and resampling until $E$ does not occur realizes $Q=P(\cdot\mid E^c)$, with $\KL(Q\Vert P)=-\log_2(1-p)=d_{\mathrm{Ber}}(0\Vert p)$ bits and $1/(1-p)$ expected generations.
\end{corollary}
\noindent The bound is attained at $Q(E)=0$ by a rule that is not a streaming decoder: the whole sequence is generated before it is accepted. When streaming is not required it is the reference against which any loop-suppressing decoder can be compared.

\section{An Exact Comparison in a Small System}
\label{sec:synthetic}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Construction}
A 20-state Markov chain has a diffuse ``safe'' region from which a small probability (0.05) leads to a \emph{gateway} state, which is not itself an immediate repeat but transitions with high probability (0.75) into a tight 2-cycle (0.80 self-reinforcing probability in either direction). The chain starts in state 0 with trailing window $(0)$. A loop is the entry of a state already in the trailing window of the last $W{=}2$ states, and the horizon is $H{=}6$ transitions throughout: every loop probability $\epsilon$ in this section is the probability of a loop within these 6 transitions. The event-to-go hazard is computed by exact backward dynamic programming over (state, trailing window, steps remaining): $f[r][(s,\text{win})]=\sum_{s'}P[s,s']\big(\mathbf{1}[s'\in\text{win}]+\mathbf{1}[s'\notin\text{win}]\,f[r{-}1][(s',\text{win}')]\big)$, with $f[0][\cdot]{=}0$, and $H(h,v)$ is $1$ if $v$ is already in the window and $f$ at the remaining horizon otherwise. The loop probability of the unmodified chain is $p=f[H][(0,(0))]=0.690024$, computed exactly. A loop is absorbing: a trajectory that loops at step $t$ pays the KL of the decisions up to and including the one that caused the loop, and nothing afterward. Every ``KL/step'' figure in this section is the exact expected total path divergence over the $H$ transitions divided by $H$, in bits; the same per-step normalization is used for the real-model tables, where the divisor is the fixed generation length.

\subsection{The optimum and the local controllers}
\label{ssec:oracle}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:min} the event-level optimum for a target $\epsilon$ costs $d_{\mathrm{Ber}}(\epsilon\Vert p)$ bits in total. We also compute it as the sequential kernel of Corollary~\ref{cor:affine}, by backward recursion with the multiplier $\lambda$ bisected until the exactly forward-computed loop probability equals $\epsilon$. The two computations of its KL agree to $<10^{-14}$ at all six grid points (for example $0.7369456415$ bits for $\epsilon{=}0.20$ and $1.5974337688$ bits for $\epsilon{=}0.01$), and the identity $g[r][(s,\text{win})]=1-(1-2^{-\lambda})f[r][(s,\text{win})]$ underlying Corollary~\ref{cor:affine} holds to $<10^{-14}$ across all 58{,}940 nodes of the $(r,s,\text{win})$ table. Residuals of the achieved loop probability against the target are below $10^{-10}$.

Two local surrogate controllers are compared with it. Each solves $\min_q\KL(q\Vert P(\cdot\mid h))$ subject to $\E_q[r]\le\varepsilon_{\text{step}}$ at every step: \emph{local} with $r$ the repeat indicator, and \emph{hazard-informed} with $r$ the exact hazard $H(h,v)$. The one-step budget $\varepsilon_{\text{step}}$ bounds a one-step expectation, whereas the optimum's $\epsilon$ bounds a sequence-level probability, so $\varepsilon_{\text{step}}$ is bisected, by exact forward propagation and without Monte Carlo, until each controller's own exact loop probability equals the target $\epsilon$. All three mechanisms are then compared at the same sequence-level operating point. The solver's feasibility flag is tracked: the hazard-informed controller's one-step budget is structurally below its achievable floor at 33--50\% of the probability mass, whereas the indicator controller is never infeasible.

\subsection{What accounts for the gap}
\label{ssec:gap}
The hazard-informed controller and the optimal rule use the same exact hazard. They differ in two ways: the optimal rule tilts affinely, $[1-(1-2^{-\lambda})H]$, and the controller exponentially, $2^{-\lambda H}$; and the optimal rule uses one multiplier $\lambda$ for the whole generation, whereas the controller re-solves a fixed one-step budget independently at every step. To separate the two we add a third rule: the exponential tilt with a single global multiplier, bisected to the same target.

\section{A Real-Model Analysis}
\label{sec:realmodel}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Setup}
\label{ssec:setup}
The primary model is an 848K-parameter, 4-layer, 128-dimensional character-level Transformer with a 256-character context, trained on Austen and Shelley (checkpoint \textsc{cosine}; Appendix~\ref{sec:protocol}). Every experiment uses the 15 \textsc{Test} prompts (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}), 10 seeds per prompt, generations of exactly 500 characters, and temperature $T{=}0.10$, the regime in which this model loops: in a 1000-character temperature sweep the loop rate falls from 96\% at $T{=}0.05$ to 0\% by $T{=}0.30$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:protocol}). The reference $P$ is plain temperature sampling at $T{=}0.10$. Decoders:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Recurrence-risk decoding}, with $\risk$ over $n$-gram orders 3--6: \textsc{rr-dual} ($\varepsilon{=}0.05$ and $0.01$), which solves the one-step problem (P) exactly at every step; \textsc{rr-fixed} ($\lambda{=}2$), which solves the penalized problem (R); and \textsc{rr-adaptive}, which sets $\lambda$ heuristically.
  \item \emph{Baselines}: repetition penalty (1.3); hard no-repeat 4-gram blocking; \textsc{SuffixMatch} ($\alpha{=}3$); an FSD-style decoder and an LZ Penalty, both adapted from the published methods (Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}).
\end{itemize}
Hard no-repeat blocking forbids the measured event by construction, so its zero loops are not an experimental finding and it is excluded from every ranking and test (Appendix~\ref{ssec:hard-repeat}).

The loop event is the earliest position at which a span of length $P\in[2,60]$ repeats five times consecutively, with a separate threshold of 8 for single-character runs. It is validated on seven held-out corpora with a pooled false-positive rate of 1.3\% (95\% CI [0.9\%, 1.9\%]), concentrated in one corpus (\emph{Dracula}, 8.7\%, epistolary formulas), and it is purely syntactic (Appendix~\ref{sec:event}). Loop counts below are counts of generations in which the detector fires, with the false-positive caveat attached.

\subsection{Event-level analysis}
\label{ssec:eventlevel}
Section~\ref{ssec:bernoulli-bound} splits a decoder's path divergence into the term $d_{\mathrm{Ber}}(Q(E)\Vert P(E))$ needed to move the loop probability and a remainder, the distortion of the conditional shape of the generation on each side of the event. We estimate both for every decoder from sample-level records of the run behind Table~\ref{tab:commonkl} (\texttt{common\_\allowbreak{}kl\_\allowbreak{}samples.csv}: for each generation, its prompt, loop event, and total path KL).

\textbf{The comparison is conditioned on the prompt.} The decomposition applies prompt by prompt, $\KL(Q_x\Vert P_x)\ge d_{\mathrm{Ber}}(Q_x(E)\Vert P_x(E))$, and the quantities reported are averages over the 15 prompts with fixed weights $1/15$. Pooling the prompts first would use the pooled loop rates, and by joint convexity of $d_{\mathrm{Ber}}$ that can only lower the bound, which overstates the distortion that is not needed. The reference loop count differs by prompt (1 to 6 of 10 generations), so the distinction is real, but it is small here: at a decoder loop fraction of zero the plug-in event term is 0.528 bits prompt by prompt and 0.487 bits pooled.

\textbf{Estimation.} $P_x(E)$ and $Q_x(E)$ are loop fractions over the 10 seeds of one prompt. The plug-in $d_{\mathrm{Ber}}$ is infinite whenever a fraction is 0 or 1 and the other is not, so the primary estimate smooths both fractions as $(k+\tfrac12)/(n+1)$; plug-in and Laplace ($(k+1)/(n+2)$) smoothings are reported as a sensitivity range. Intervals are percentile intervals from resampling whole prompts with replacement (10{,}000 resamples, the same prompts for the reference and the decoder), which keeps every generation's loop event and path KL together. Everything is a sample estimate from 150 generations per decoder; the output is descriptive and does not have guaranteed coverage.

\textbf{Reading.} For every decoder the estimated event term lies between 0.20 and 0.53 bits under all three smoothings, whereas the measured path KL ranges from 19 to over 6000 bits, so at least 98\% of what each decoder spends is estimated to be conditional-shape distortion and not the cost of moving the loop probability. The excess is smallest for \textsc{rr-fixed} (98.4\%) and above 99.9\% for FSD-style, hard no-repeat blocking, and \textsc{rr-dual}. The event term itself is uncertain by about $\pm0.15$ bits because it rests on 10 generations per prompt, which does not affect this reading. It would matter if a decoder's excess were of the same order as the event term, and none is.

This is not a bound on what a practical decoder could save. The event-level optimum needs the event-to-go hazard, which cannot be evaluated exactly on a real model (Appendix~\ref{sec:hazard-transfer}), the detector's event is syntactic, and the analysis is for one model at one temperature. The reference point that does not need the hazard is rejection of whole generations (Section~\ref{ssec:alternatives}): prompt by prompt it costs $-\log_2(1-P_x(E))$ bits and $1/(1-P_x(E))$ expected generations, on average 0.56 bits and 1.5 generations under the same smoothing (0.49 bits and 1.4 generations from the pooled loop rate of 28.7\%).


\subsection{Other models}
\label{ssec:othermodels}
The same decoders were run on a second character-level model trained on non-fiction (Darwin, Smith; Thoreau for validation, Machiavelli for test), on GPT-2, and on OLMo-2-0425-1B and Mistral-7B-v0.3 (Appendix~\ref{sec:generalization}); Qwen2.5-3B showed no repetition regime at the budget tested and is not compared. Two observations carry over and one does not. The dual mode's per-step feasibility was exactly 100.0\% on every model, as it should be for a one-step problem solved exactly, and it reached 0 observed loops in every setting tested, at much higher per-step KL than the history-based baselines. The heuristics' effectiveness, however, varies from model to model: \textsc{rr-fixed} shows 4/150 loops on the primary model and 77/150 on the non-fiction model, \textsc{rr-adaptive} lowers the loop count on OLMo (6/50 to 1/50) but is indistinguishable from the reference on Mistral, and the LZ Penalty and FSD-style decoder reach zero observed loops on some models and not on others. The prompt-conditional analysis above was run on the primary model only, the only model for which sample-level path KL is stored.

\section{Discussion}
\label{ssec:discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{What the analysis supports}
\textbf{The gap in the small system is exact, and its source is identified.} With the exact multi-step hazard as its risk signal, a controller that re-solves a fixed one-step budget at every step needs 1.6--3.3 times the optimal KL for the same loop probability, and one that uses a repeat indicator needs 2.3--5.1 times (Section~\ref{ssec:oracle}). The ablation of Section~\ref{ssec:gap} localizes the gap: using the hazard in an exponential instead of the optimal affine form costs at most about 10\%, while re-solving a fixed budget at every step costs the rest. The optimum is itself causal, so per-step control is not inefficient because it is causal; it is inefficient because a per-step budget cannot share one distortion budget across the generation.

\textbf{On the real model, the distortion that decoders spend is not mainly spent on moving the loop probability.} Prompt-conditional plug-in estimates (Section~\ref{ssec:eventlevel}) put the event term of every decoder between 0.2 and 0.5 bits, against 19 to 6300 bits of measured path KL, so at least 98\% of what the decoders spend is estimated to be distortion of the conditional shape of the generation and not the cost of moving the loop probability. The conclusion is descriptive: it rests on 15 prompts and 10 seeds, the event term depends on how the 10-seed fractions are smoothed (the range is reported), and the optimum cannot be attained without the hazard.

\textbf{The dual mode's guarantee is local.} It bounds the expected recurrence risk at each step whenever the numerical projection succeeds, and that held at every step on every model tested. It does not bound the probability of a loop: every zero in this paper is an observed count of a detector firing in 50--150 generations at one temperature.

\subsection{What it does not support}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The estimates of Section~\ref{ssec:eventlevel} are descriptive. They do not say how much distortion a practical decoder could save: the event-level optimum needs the event-to-go hazard, which on a real model can only be estimated: a rollout estimate separates risky from novel candidates (Appendix~\ref{sec:hazard-transfer}), but no decoder built on it has been evaluated, and the estimated event term is itself uncertain.
  \item Recurrence-risk decoding is not shown to improve survival over baselines at ordinary temperatures, where the primary model does not loop, and the dual mode is not proposed as a practical decoder.
  \item High KL is not evidence of poor readability. A pilot human evaluation (Appendix~\ref{sec:human-eval}, $n{=}9$) detected no reliable readability difference among four decoders whose per-step KL differs by a factor of about 70, which neither supports nor contradicts a cost of distortion.
  \item The heuristics' effectiveness is model-specific (Section~\ref{ssec:othermodels}); nothing here shows it is a property of ``modern models'' as a category, and Qwen2.5-3B showed no repetition regime at the budget tested.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Alternatives to streaming control}
\label{ssec:alternatives}
When generation need not be streamed, rejecting a whole generation in which the detector fires and drawing a new one attains the event-level optimum for $Q(E)=0$ exactly (Corollary~\ref{cor:reject}): the accepted samples follow $P(\cdot\mid E^c)$, at a KL of $-\log_2(1-p)$ bits and $1/(1-p)$ expected generations. At the primary model's reference loop rate of 28.7\% this is 0.49 bits and 1.4 generations per accepted sample, against 0.04--12.7 bits per step (about 19--6300 bits per generation) for the decoders of Table~\ref{tab:pcb}. Its cost grows as $1/(1-p)$, so it is unattractive when almost every generation loops (greedy decoding loops in 140 of 150), it needs a detector that can be evaluated on a completed generation, and it samples from the model conditioned on the detector's event not firing, which is only as good as the detector (Appendix~\ref{sec:event}). Sequence-aware causal control with an estimated hazard is the streaming counterpart; a rollout estimate of the hazard carries signal (Appendix~\ref{sec:hazard-transfer}), but turning it into a calibrated per-step rule is not attempted here.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Detector.} The pooled false-positive rate of about 1.3\% conceals per-corpus variance: \emph{Dracula} alone fires at 26/300 (8.7\%), about seven times the pooled figure, in epistolary formulas that a syntactic detector cannot distinguish from degeneration (Table~\ref{tab:validation}). The non-fiction corpus's 0.8\% was not checked for single-corpus concentration, and the calibration of the subword detectors did not meet the pre-declared 1\% tolerance (Appendix~\ref{sec:generalization}). The detector is syntactic and does not capture semantic repetition; every loop count carries these caveats.
  \item \textbf{Units and numerical tolerances.} All KL values are in bits and all per-step values are means over the fixed generation length (Section~\ref{sec:formal}). The tolerances of the dual solver, the float32 computation, and the exact-computation residuals are stated in Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}.
  \item \textbf{Statistical scope.} One temperature ($T{=}0.10$), 15 prompts and 10 seeds on the primary model. Prompt-level quantities rest on 10 generations per prompt, the event-level terms are plug-in estimates under stated smoothing, and nothing in Section~\ref{ssec:eventlevel} is a bound with guaranteed coverage.
  \item \textbf{Models.} The prompt-conditional analysis uses one character-level model of 848K parameters. The other models are compared at a compact scale (50--150 generations per configuration, Appendix~\ref{sec:generalization}), with no sample-level KL records.
  \item \textbf{Baselines.} FSD-style and LZ Penalty are adapted from the published methods and are not reproductions (Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}); \textsc{SuffixMatch} is a homemade heuristic.
\end{itemize}

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================

Per-step repetition control can be inefficient for preventing a loop across the whole sequence because a one-step budget is the wrong unit: the distortion needed to move the probability of a loop is the Bernoulli KL $d_{\mathrm{Ber}}(\epsilon\Vert p)$, attained by reweighting whole paths by whether they loop, and a rule that re-solves a fixed per-step budget cannot allocate its distortion that way even when it is handed the exact hazard. In a finite-state system this costs 1.6--5.1 times the optimal KL; the optimal rule is causal when the hazard is available, so the loss is in the allocation of distortion, not in causality. On a real model, conditioning on the prompt, at least 98\% of the path KL of every decoder tested is estimated to be distortion that does not move the loop probability (descriptive plug-in estimates, 15 prompts, one temperature). The dual mode of recurrence-risk decoding solves the one-step problem exactly at every step on every model tested; that is a statement about the one-step problem and not about the probability of a loop, which is observed, not guaranteed. What remains open is a sequence-aware causal decoder for real text: the event-to-go hazard has to be estimated, a rollout estimate separates risky from novel candidates, and no decoder built on it has been evaluated. And, when streaming is not needed, rejection of loop-containing samples is already optimal for the event the detector defines.

%==============================================================================

\subsection{Why the naive event fails}
\label{ssec:naive-fails}
The candidate event ``the first repeated $n$-gram of length $n$'' fires on 54.0\% of held-out Doyle windows and 72.0\% of held-out Dickens windows at $n{=}10$. An event that fires on the majority of ordinary prose cannot distinguish degeneration from fluency and is not used.

\subsection{Calibration}
The persistent-cycle event is the earliest position $e$ at which a span of length $P\in[2,P_{\max}]$ repeats exactly $R$ times consecutively. $R$ and $P_{\max}$ are chosen by a pre-declared rule (the smallest $R$ whose worst-case upper 95\% CI, across window lengths 300/500/1000/2000, falls at or below a 1\% tolerance) on a calibration set (Carroll, Twain, Swift) never referenced afterward. No cell meets the 1\% tolerance at every window length; $R{=}6$ reproduces $R{=}5$'s counts exactly, indicating that the residual is stable and not a threshold-boundary artifact. Frozen: $R{=}5$, $P_{\max}{=}60$, with worst-case upper CI 2.80\%, which does not meet the a priori target.

Period 1 (a single repeated character) uses a separate, decoupled threshold of 8. Using $R{=}5$ for period 1 caused plain-text typesetting conventions (five-character dash dividers) to fire; the threshold of 8 sits above ordinary divider and ellipsis lengths and below observed degenerate runs. Every other period $P\in[2,60]$ uses $R{=}5$.

\section{Experimental Protocol and Additional Primary-Model Results}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Model and data}
The primary model is an 848K-parameter, 4-layer, 128-dimensional character-level Transformer with a 256-character context, trained on Austen and Shelley, validated on Doyle, and tested on Dickens. All primary-model results use the \textsc{cosine}-schedule checkpoint unless stated otherwise. A second, independently trained checkpoint of the same architecture and data (\textsc{baseline}, constant learning rate) provides a replication: greedy decoding loops at 60.0\% (vs.\ 93.3\% for \textsc{cosine}) and $T{=}0.15$ still shows a 4.0\% loop rate where \textsc{cosine} shows 0.0\%, so the temperature threshold does not carry over even across retrains of the same architecture and data. \textsc{rr-dual} ($\varepsilon{=}0.05$) reaches 0/150 loops on \textsc{baseline} at 100.0\% feasibility and 11.343 bits of KL, close to \textsc{cosine}'s 11.186 bits (Table~\ref{tab:main}).

\subsection{Where the pathology occurs}
A sweep of 14 temperatures $\times$ 5 prompts $\times$ 10 seeds (1000-character generations, exact Clopper--Pearson intervals) shows the loop rate falling smoothly from 96.0\% at $T{=}0.05$ to 0.0\% by $T{=}0.30$, crossing below 5\% near $T{\approx}0.2$. Every primary-model decoding comparison runs at $T{=}0.10$. The sweep was not repeated for the second model (Section~\ref{sec:generalization}), whose safe temperature range does not simply transfer.

\subsection{Prompt protocol}
A \textsc{Dev} set of 5 prompts is the only material inspected while choosing hyperparameters. A disjoint \textsc{Test} set of 15 prompts, written afterward and not inspected during tuning, is the source of every number in Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:generalization}. All configurations, greedy decoding included, are evaluated on all 15 test prompts.

\subsection{Inference}
\label{ssec:inference}
The unit of analysis for every confidence interval and hypothesis test is the \emph{prompt}. Two procedures were declared in advance:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Primary: exact sign-flip test.} For 15 prompts, all $2^{15}$ sign patterns are enumerated, giving an exact $p$-value for the paired difference in prompt-level RMST (\texttt{compute\_\allowbreak{}significance.py}, which reproduces every number in Table~\ref{tab:paired}).
  \item \textbf{Sensitivity: cluster bootstrap.} 3000 resamples of prompts give a 95\% CI for the same statistic.
\end{itemize}
At small $n$ the two need not agree; Section~\ref{ssec:disagreement} reports where they differ. The primary test governs.

\subsection{Metrics}
\textbf{RMST} has a horizon of 500 characters and uses Kaplan--Meier with right-censoring. Model-consistency NLL (mcNLL) scores every target position independently with its exact context window $\text{full}[\max(0,t{-}\text{block\_size}):t]$, batched with right-padding so that causal masking cannot leak filler into real positions (Appendix~\ref{app:defects}). 4-gram similarity is measured against a held-out reference corpus. For dual-calibrated configurations the exact $\KL(q\Vert p)$, achieved risk, and per-step feasibility diagnostics are reported. Every $\KL(q\Vert p)$ figure reported anywhere in this paper for a real model, including Tables~\ref{tab:main}, \ref{tab:commonkl}, \ref{tab:olmofull}, and \ref{tab:mistralfull}, is a \emph{per-step mean}: the divergence $\KL(q_t\Vert p_t)$ between that step's sampling distribution and the shared reference is computed at every generated position, averaged over the fixed generation length of that sample (500 characters for the primary and non-fiction models, 200 tokens for GPT-2, 150 for OLMo and Mistral), and then averaged again over samples. Generation always runs for the full fixed length regardless of whether or when a loop is detected, so a sample's total path divergence is exactly its reported per-step mean multiplied by that fixed length.

\subsection{No-repeat blocking is excluded from ranking}
\label{ssec:hard-repeat}
A global no-repeat-$n$-gram filter, checked against the entire history, structurally cannot sustain a persistent cycle of any period $P$: once a period-$P$ cycle has run for at least $n$ steps, every subsequent $n$-gram repeats one produced $P$ steps earlier and is blocked, regardless of the relation between $P$ and $n$. Its 0/150 result holds by construction and is not an experimental finding, so it is excluded from every significance test and from the survival ranking.

\section{Baselines and Dual-Solver Details}
\label{app:baselines}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Dual calibration}
\label{ssec:dual-fix}
The solver works entirely in log-space: $p$ is never materialized as a probability tensor; $q_\lambda$'s logits are $\log p-\lambda\risk$ computed from \texttt{F.log\_\allowbreak{}softmax} of the temperature-scaled logits, and $\KL(q\Vert p)$ and both entropies are computed from log-probabilities, with $0\log0:=0$ and a conversion to bits (division by $\ln2$). This matters at low temperature: at $T{=}0.10$ many vocabulary entries have probabilities below the float32 range, and materializing $p$ would collapse distinct low-probability tokens to a single floor value.

Structural infeasibility is decided in closed form before any search: if $\min_v\risk(v)>\varepsilon$ (with tolerance), the step is reported as \texttt{structurally\_\allowbreak{}infeasible} and no search is attempted. Otherwise a feasible $\lambda$ exists, and the search bracket expands geometrically (doubling from $\lambda{=}1$, at most 60 doublings, so $\lambda\le2^{60}\approx1.15\times10^{18}$) until its upper end is feasible, and then 30 bisection iterations are run within it. The limit of 60 doublings is practical and not mathematical; Section~\ref{ssec:lambda-estimate} shows that it is far beyond anything a risk-weighted logit shift requires away from the boundary case.

\textbf{Numerical tolerances.} Risk values are float32 and $\varepsilon$ is float64, so the comparison of $\min_v\risk(v)$ with $\varepsilon$ uses an absolute tolerance of $10^{-6}$ (in risk units), and a step with $|\min_v\risk(v)-\varepsilon|\le10^{-6}$ is flagged \texttt{near\_\allowbreak{}boundary}, which is distinct from both an interior point and structural infeasibility; in this case the feasibility target is relaxed by the same tolerance. Per-step diagnostics (feasibility status, $\lambda$, achieved risk, violation, final bracket width, number of doublings, and the boundary flag) are saved for every dual-mode configuration. On the primary model the dual solver was feasible at 100.0\% of steps for both $\varepsilon$, with zero mean and zero maximum violation at the stored precision of four decimals, a mean of about 2.2 bracket doublings per step and a maximum of 9, and no near-boundary or structurally infeasible step. In the synthetic system the one-step solver bisects $\lambda\in[0,200]$ for 40 iterations, with feasibility checked in closed form against the minimum risk among states with positive prior mass (tolerance $10^{-12}$), and the oracle and the exact forward propagations carry residuals below $10^{-10}$.

\subsection{Recurrence risk and prompt context}
$\risk(v)$ uses an incrementally maintained hash map, checked to be identical to brute-force history scanning. Whether the prompt's own tokens count as history was tested (\texttt{include\_\allowbreak{}prompt\_\allowbreak{}context}$\in\{$True,False$\}$): the RMST difference (492.9 vs.\ 499.9) is not significant under the primary exact test ($p{=}0.125$).

\subsection{The adaptive controller}
$\lambda_t=\lambda_{\text{base}}+\lambda_{\text{rep}}\max(0,\overline{\text{rep}}-\text{rep}_{\text{target}})-\lambda_{\text{ent}}\max(0,\overline{H}-H_{\text{target}})$, clipped to $[0,\lambda_{\max}]$. The lower clip is a correctness requirement, since an unclipped negative multiplier would reward risk.

\subsection{SuffixMatch}
\textsc{SuffixMatch} finds the longest suffix of the generated history (last 400 tokens) that occurred earlier and subtracts $\alpha\cdot\text{match\_len}/20$ from the logits of the tokens that followed those earlier occurrences, with $\alpha{=}3$; it scans the history at every step, at cost $O(\text{history})$. It is a homemade longest-suffix-match heuristic inspired by LZ77, not the published Look-back decoding algorithm and not attributed to any publication.

\subsection{FSD-style decoder}
\label{app:fsd}
Frustratingly Simple Decoding \citep{yang2023frustratingly} contrasts the model with a training-free $n$-gram predictor built from the text generated so far (the anti-LM). The authors' code is at github.com/LHRYANG/FSD; the version used for comparison is commit \texttt{46df7d5bc622\allowbreak{}8bd5045b68c0\allowbreak{}9f85d75bcceb\allowbreak{}1fdf} (\texttt{ngram\_model/fsd.py} last changed in \texttt{0f469ac54f26\allowbreak{}b02d20f831a8\allowbreak{}27c09336ede5\allowbreak{}f06a}, 2024-02-23). At that commit \texttt{utils.py} calls its \texttt{NGram} objects directly, which the same commit's \texttt{fsd.py} does not define, so the penalty is checked against \texttt{NGram.penalize}, the function that computes it.

The FSD-style decoder here follows the official scoring. Candidates are the top $k{=}6$ tokens by $p_\theta$ and are ranked by $p_\theta(v)-\alpha\,p_\omega(v)$ with $\alpha{=}3$ (the official code writes $(1{-}a)p_\theta-a\,p_\omega$ with $a=\alpha/(1{+}\alpha)$, which has the same argmax). The anti-LM $p_\omega$ is the recursive residual-mass construction: for a candidate, orders are visited from the highest ($N{=}3$, context length 2) down to the unigram, with $\text{remaining}=1$; an order at which the candidate never followed the current context is skipped and leaves its residual to the lower orders; an order at which it did contributes $\text{remaining}\cdot\beta\cdot\text{count}/(\text{total}{+}1)$ ($\beta{=}0.9$), and the unigram contributes $\text{remaining}\cdot\text{count}/\text{total}$ without $\beta$; after each contributing order $\text{remaining}\leftarrow\text{remaining}(1-\beta)$. When every order contributes the weights are about $0.90/0.09/0.01$. The anti-LM scores of this decoder equal those of \texttt{NGram.penalize} to within $10^{-9}$ on constructed prefixes and 300 random histories (\texttt{tests/test\_\allowbreak{}fsd\_\allowbreak{}against\_\allowbreak{}official.py}, which fetches the pinned file at run time because the repository carries no license).

It departs from the official method in two respects, so it is labeled FSD-style (adapted) and is not presented as a reproduction. The official method takes the argmax over the top-$k$ scores, whereas this study resamples every configuration over several seeds, so the decoder samples from the top-$k$ scores after clamping at zero and renormalizing (from $p_\theta$'s own top-$k$ if every score clamps to zero). And $p_\theta$ is the temperature-scaled softmax that serves as the reference distribution throughout, where the official code uses the raw softmax.

\subsection{LZ Penalty}
\label{app:lz}
The LZ Penalty \citep{ginart2025lzpenalty} penalizes each candidate token by the change in codelength it would induce under an LZ-style compressor applied to a recent buffer against a lookback window. The reference implementation is github.com/tginart/sglang, \texttt{python/\allowbreak{}sglang/\allowbreak{}srt/\allowbreak{}sampling/\allowbreak{}penaltylib/\allowbreak{}lz\_\allowbreak{}penalty.py} at commit \texttt{ab08e6976d49\allowbreak{}80e350df3891\allowbreak{}e40967a9718f\allowbreak{}ae8b} (2025-07-01, Apache-2.0). For a context $W$ and buffer length $n$, the penalty vector of its \texttt{compute\_lz\_penalty\_stateless} is $\log_2V-\min\{\max\{\log_2 d(a),0\},\log_2V\}$ for every candidate $a$, where $d(a)$ is the distance to the closest position of the context at which $a$ follows the longest match of the buffer's trailing tokens ($+\infty$, hence penalty $0$, if $a$ never occurred), and the reference subtracts it from the logits scaled by the user weight. This study adds $\alpha\cdot(\text{cost}-\log_2V)$ to the logits, the same quantity with the opposite sign.

\texttt{LZPenaltyDecoder.\_penalty\_vector} equals the reference function exactly (maximum difference $0.0$) on constructed and 300 random contexts (\texttt{tests/test\_\allowbreak{}lz\_\allowbreak{}against\_\allowbreak{}reference.py}). Two compatibility notes apply to that test. The reference casts an integer tensor with \texttt{torch.full\_like(x, inf)}, which raises on current PyTorch, so the extracted function is run with an added float cast. And its early return for a context no longer than $n$ passes a tensor as a size to \texttt{torch.zeros} and raises, so that branch is checked on this side only (an all-zero penalty).

The decoding path differs from the reference's state handling, so this decoder is labeled LZ Penalty (adapted): the reference keeps only generated tokens in a window of \texttt{lz\_\allowbreak{}lookback\_\allowbreak{}size}, whereas this implementation keeps the prompt and the generated tokens and uses the last \texttt{buffer\_\allowbreak{}size}$+$\texttt{window\_\allowbreak{}size} of them. Buffer and window are 8 and 128 with $\alpha{=}0.15$ for the character model, and 32 and 512 for the subword models, scaled down for the character vocabulary, and the penalty is applied to the logits before temperature scaling.

\subsection{Order-of-magnitude estimate for the multiplier cap}
\label{ssec:lambda-estimate}
\label{app:lambda-estimate}
For a two-outcome simplification (one risky token with $\risk{=}1$, one safe token with $\risk{=}0$, raw pre-temperature probability ratio $\rho$ risky:safe), the $\lambda$ needed to bring $q(\text{risky})\le\varepsilon$ at temperature $T$ is $\lambda\ge\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{T}\ln\rho$. At $T{=}0.10$, $\rho{=}100$ needs $\lambda\approx50$ and $\rho{=}1000$ needs $\lambda\approx73$. This is for an interior point, away from the $\varepsilon=\min_v\risk(v)$ boundary, where the required $\lambda$ depends on how small a float64 gap the solver must resolve against a float32-rounded risk floor and is handled by the \texttt{near\_\allowbreak{}boundary} tolerance. The 60-doubling limit exceeds the $\rho{=}1000$ estimate by roughly 16 orders of magnitude and is not observed to bind outside the boundary case.

\subsection{What transfers and what does not}
\textbf{The per-step feasibility rate is consistent across models; zero persistent loops is an empirical observation, not a proven consequence.} \textsc{rr-dual} reaches exactly 100.0\% per-step feasibility on this corpus at both $\varepsilon$, identical to the primary model. Feasibility is the quantity the theorem governs: it bounds the per-step \emph{expected} recurrence risk when the numerical projection succeeds, and its success rate stays consistent across corpora because $\lambda$ is re-solved from $p$ and $\risk$ at every step, with no corpus-tuned constant. The theorem says nothing about the sequence-level count of persistent loops, so 0/150 on both models is reported as a repeated empirical observation.

\textbf{The fixed-hyperparameter modes do not transfer.} \textsc{rr-fixed} ($\lambda{=}2$, unchanged) rises from 2.7\% loops on the primary model to 51.3\% here, and \textsc{rr-adaptive} from 0.0\% to 21.3\%. The gap between \textsc{rr-dual} and \textsc{rr-fixed} on this corpus is large and highly significant (paired exact test, $\Delta$RMST $=+60.8$, $p{=}0.0001$). A fixed penalty strength suited to one corpus's risk and entropy statistics does not carry its effectiveness to a corpus with different repetition statistics, although the code and hyperparameters are identical. \textsc{rr-fixed}'s output here remains visibly cyclical (\emph{``the same manner as the produce of the country, the country which they have been sufficient to the produce of the same manner, and the profits of the same manner as the produce of the produce of the land and labour of the produce of the produce of''}).

\textbf{The primary model's temperature-safe regime does not transfer.} $T{=}0.15$, which reached 0.0\% loops on the primary model, still shows 26.0\% here. The temperature sweep was not repeated for this model, so its safe threshold is unknown.

Repetition penalty and \textsc{rr-adaptive} weaken substantially on this corpus (36.0\% and 21.3\% loops, from 4.7\% and 0.0\%). Among the history-aware and hard-constraint mechanisms, effectiveness transfers unevenly: SuffixMatch and hard no-repeat still reach 0/150, whereas FSD-style rises to 16.0\% (from 2.0\%) and LZ Penalty to 2.0\% (from 0.0\%). LZ Penalty's effectiveness also does not carry over to GPT-2 (0.0\% to 8.0\%). For FSD-style and LZ Penalty, unlike SuffixMatch and hard no-repeat, effectiveness is therefore a property of the model and corpus and not of the mechanism's design.

\textbf{The adaptive controller's benefit is model-dependent.} On OLMo, \textsc{rr-adaptive} cuts the loop rate from the 12.0\% shared by the baseline and \textsc{rr-fixed} to 2.0\% at negligible KL cost (0.027 bits). This is not an artifact of the calibrated threshold: across a full $(R,P_{\max})$ sensitivity sweep (5 values of $R\in\{4,\ldots,8\}\times5$ values of $P_{\max}\in\{10,\ldots,40\}$, 25 grid points, using the raw generated token ids saved for this purpose), the ordering $\textsc{rr-adaptive}\le\textsc{rr-fixed}\le$ baseline holds at every grid point, with 0 reversals out of 25. On Mistral, \textsc{rr-adaptive} and \textsc{rr-fixed} are indistinguishable from the $T{=}0.10$ baseline at the frozen threshold (4.0\% for all three), and the same sweep shows no consistent direction: at looser thresholds ($R{=}3,4$) both sit below the baseline, at the calibrated and stricter thresholds ($R{=}5$--$7$, tight $P_{\max}$) at or slightly above it (e.g.\ $R{=}5$, $P_{\max}{=}10$: baseline 0.0\%, both variants 4.0\%). \textbf{The cheapest route to zero measured loops differs by model.} On OLMo, SuffixMatch is the cheapest of the methods with zero observed loops (0.223 bits), whereas on the primary model LZ Penalty is (Table~\ref{tab:commonkl}). GPT-2 has no comparable KL ranking (loop rate, RMST, and mcNLL only), and SuffixMatch does not reach zero loops there (4.0\%). On Mistral, none of SuffixMatch (4.0\%), LZ Penalty (2.0\%), or FSD-style (2.0\%) reaches zero loops; among the external baselines only repetition penalty (8.059 bits) and the hard no-repeat constraint (4.500 bits) do. \textsc{rr-dual} remains the most expensive route to zero loops on both models (4.39--4.59 bits on OLMo, 6.74--7.26 bits on Mistral). The detector-threshold sweep covered a seven-configuration subset (the recurrence-risk family, SuffixMatch, and FSD-style); on OLMo, SuffixMatch and both dual configurations stay at exactly 0.0\% at every one of the 25 grid points, and on Mistral SuffixMatch's loop rate ranges 4.0--20.0\% across the 25 cells, consistent with the 4.0\% at the calibrated point. The sweep for FSD-style used an earlier implementation and is not reported, and LZ Penalty was not included.

\textbf{Generation length does not hide a different picture at 150 tokens.} Re-censoring each sample's saved onset at shorter horizons (30, 60, 90, 120 tokens) shows OLMo's \textsc{rr-adaptive} advantage opening in the back half of generation (0.0\% for both \textsc{rr-adaptive} and baseline through 90 tokens; the baseline reaches 10--12\% by 150 tokens against 0--2\% for \textsc{rr-adaptive}). The Mistral null finding holds at every horizon tested.

\textsc{rr-dual} reaches exactly 100.0\% per-step feasibility on both OLMo and Mistral (both $\varepsilon$, zero structurally infeasible and zero near-boundary steps), so the guarantee has held without exception on every model tested: the primary character model and its second checkpoint, the non-fiction model, GPT-2, OLMo, and Mistral. Together with the GPT-2 character-level $(R,P_{\max})$ sensitivity check (25 grid points, all zero-loop findings unchanged), this shows that a mechanism's effectiveness, unlike the dual mechanism's per-step feasibility, is an empirical property of a specific model and cannot be assumed to transfer from one modern checkpoint to the next.

\section{Pilot Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:human-eval}
%==============================================================================

This pilot asks whether human readers can tell the decoders apart at the temperature of the main experiments. It is not part of the paper's argument: KL is not used as a proxy for readability anywhere in the text.

\subsection{Design}
A blinded, self-contained offline page (\texttt{human\_\allowbreak{}eval\_\allowbreak{}tool.html}, with an English or Russian interface) has two tasks: (1) loop-detection agreement on 18 passages (clearly looping, clearly non-looping, and borderline), and (2) blind pairwise preference on 12 pairs covering all six pairings among four decoders: \textsc{dual}, SuffixMatch, and LZ Penalty, which reach 0/150 observed loops on the primary model, and FSD-style, which does not. The tool randomizes presentation order and left/right side independently per participant and ends with a copy-to-clipboard export of the raw responses (\texttt{aggregate\_\allowbreak{}human\_\allowbreak{}eval.py} combines the exports). Task 1 reports agreement with the frozen detector's classification, not with ground truth: the detector is an operational, purely syntactic instrument with a known, nonzero false-positive rate concentrated on specific corpora (Appendix~\ref{sec:event}).

\textbf{Which implementation produced the passages.} The passages were generated from the \textsc{cosine} checkpoint at $T{=}0.10$ with the decoders as they stood when the evaluation was built. The \textsc{dual}, SuffixMatch, and LZ Penalty passages are from the same code as in the tables of the main text. The FSD-style comparisons below therefore concern the intermediate version.

Nine participants were recruited informally, not through a formal panel, and sessions took a median of about 9.6 minutes (range 2--11 minutes). Each raw export was checked for internal consistency and independence before inclusion, and only sessions that passed are reported.

\section{Estimating the Hazard on the Real Model}
\label{sec:hazard-transfer}
%==============================================================================

Sequence-aware causal control needs the event-to-go hazard $H(h,v)$ (Section~\ref{ssec:bernoulli-bound}), which on a real model has to be estimated. This appendix reports a rollout-based estimator on the primary model and what it does and does not show.

\subsection{Design}
Contexts are cut from saved generations (greedy, $T{=}0.05$, $T{=}0.10$, and repetition-penalty runs): 70\% from the 100 characters preceding a detected loop onset and 30\% from uniformly random cut points. Contexts whose own text already contains the loop event are discarded, since the hazard is defined for generations that have not yet looped. For each context, 12 candidate next tokens are probed (the model's top 8 by probability and 4 lower-probability tokens). Each candidate is forced as the next token and $K{=}4$ independent stochastic continuations at $T{=}0.10$ are run for 150 characters in total. A rollout is labeled by whether the frozen detector fires on the \emph{full} text, the earlier context followed by the rollout, so that a cycle that starts in the context and completes in the rollout counts. Rows are batched only with rows of the same context length, so that no padding is ever added and every row sees exactly its own context, as in real decoding. A candidate is \emph{risky} if choosing it completes an already-seen $n$-gram ($\risk>0$) and \emph{novel} otherwise. A multi-step variant forces the candidate and then $L{-}1$ greedy steps ($L\in\{1,3,5,8,15\}$) before resuming sampling.

\subsection{Results}
\textbf{Single-token design.} After discarding contexts that already contain the event, 147 of 150 contexts remain, and 133 of them contain both risky and novel candidates (171 risky and 1593 novel (context, candidate) examples). The mean hazard label is 0.291 for risky candidates and 0.097 for novel ones. The per-context paired difference (risky minus novel, averaged over contexts) is 0.244, with a 95\% interval of $[0.188,0.303]$ from resampling whole contexts, and an exact sign-flip test (100{,}000 random sign flips) gives $p<10^{-4}$.

\textbf{Multi-step commitment.} For 50 contexts with both a risky and a novel candidate, the separation grows with the number of forced greedy steps $L$ (Table~\ref{tab:hazardprobe}); all five paired differences are significant after Bonferroni adjustment across the five values of $L$.

\textbf{What this shows and does not show.} A rollout estimate of the hazard separates candidates that complete an already-seen $n$-gram from those that do not, on this model, by a margin that is large relative to its sampling uncertainty. It does not show that such an estimate is accurate enough, or calibrated enough, to drive a decoder: each label rests on 4--5 rollouts, 70\% of the contexts are cut from the 100 characters before a detected onset, so the base rates are those of that design and not of ordinary generation, and novel candidates still carry a hazard of about 0.10. No decoder using the estimate has been built or evaluated, so the event-level optimum remains out of reach on real text for lack of a usable hazard, not for lack of a rule.

\section{Implementation Defects and Corrections}
\label{app:defects}
%==============================================================================

This appendix records defects found during development, their effect on reported numbers, and their corrections. Every table in the paper reflects the corrected code.

\subsection*{Loop detector and reproducibility}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Window-sampling seed.} The calibration and validation script seeded its window sampling with Python's built-in \texttt{hash()} on corpus names, which is randomized per process, so the sampled windows differed between invocations. It now uses a seed derived from \texttt{hashlib.md5}, verified identical across processes, and saves the sampled window start indices. Re-running reproduces the frozen setting ($R{=}5$, $P_{\max}{=}60$) and closely similar false-positive counts.
  \item \textbf{Period-1 threshold.} The detector was first described as ``$R{=}5$'' without qualification; period 1 uses a decoupled threshold of 8.
  \item \textbf{Result keys and prompts.} \texttt{compute\_\allowbreak{}significance.py} used the pre-rename key \texttt{lt\_lookback\_a3.0}, which silently dropped that comparison from every run after the class was renamed from \texttt{LookBackDecoder} (a name that implied the published Look-back algorithm) to \textsc{SuffixMatchDecoder}. Greedy decoding was initially evaluated on 5 of the 15 test prompts and is now evaluated on all 15.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Model-consistency NLL}
The first implementation scored non-overlapping chunks of length \texttt{block\_\allowbreak{}size}; on a toy model about half of all scored positions received less context than the architecture allows (in one case 9 of 16 possible context tokens). A first fix (overlapping windows, keeping each window's back half) was still insufficient. The final method scores every target position independently with its exact context window; on the toy model all 40 scored positions receive exactly $\min(t,\text{block\_size})$ tokens. An intermediate version left-padded windows with a real vocabulary token id, which unmasked causal attention attends to (0.26 maximum logit difference between isolated and padded evaluation of the same context); right-padding is used instead, since causal masking cannot let it leak into real positions, and the batched computation matches an isolated forward pass to 6 decimals.

\subsection*{Dual solver}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Float32 underflow.} The solver computed $q_\lambda$'s logits as $\log(p+10^{-40})-\lambda\risk$ from a materialized softmax. At $T{=}0.10$, 276 of 2000 tested vocabulary entries underflowed to exactly $0.0$ in one constructed check, with true log-probabilities spanning 41 nats collapsed to one floor value ($\log10^{-40}\approx-92.1$). The solver now works in log-space throughout.
  \item \textbf{Infeasibility conflated with the cap.} True infeasibility ($\varepsilon<\min_v\risk(v)$) was learned only by bisecting to a fixed cap ($\lambda_{\max}{=}50$, later 1000) and failing. It is now decided in closed form and the bracket expands geometrically (at most 60 doublings; an earlier description of the search as having ``no ceiling'' was incorrect).
  \item \textbf{Discarded diagnostics.} An earlier run hid a solver that was silently infeasible at about 26--28\% of steps.
  \item \textbf{Float32/float64 comparison at the boundary.} At $\varepsilon=\min_v\risk(v)$ the comparison could round the wrong way and misreport a feasible point as infeasible; a tolerance and a \texttt{near\_\allowbreak{}boundary} flag were added.
  \item \textbf{NaN diagnostics.} A NaN entered the KL and entropy diagnostics whenever top-$p$ filtering ran after the projection with $q(v){=}0$ exactly.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Effect.} Dual per-step KL on the primary model was 2.256--2.328 bits in the original run, 10.3--11.6 bits after the cap fix alone, and 11.2--12.7 bits after the numerical fix, about five times the original figure. Per-step feasibility was 72.3--74.5\% originally, 98.75--98.87\% after the cap fix, and is exactly 100.0\% after the numerical fix. Loop counts do not depend on this arithmetic and were unaffected.

\subsection*{Theory}
The projection theorem originally omitted the $\varepsilon=\min_v\risk(v)$ boundary, where no finite dual variable exists. The statement that the method reduces to hard no-repeat blocking as $\lambda\to\infty$ was wrong twice: it fails when $n_{\min}<n_{\max}$, and even for a single order it ignores that hard blocking fails when no safe continuation exists. A statement that no causally committed token-by-token rule can realize the event-level optimum was wrong: the affine-hazard kernel (Corollary~\ref{cor:affine}) is such a rule when the exact event-to-go hazard is available. The no-repeat argument previously stated a lag condition $P\le n$ that is not needed. And an earlier explanation of the gap in the small system attributed it to using the hazard exponentially instead of affinely; the ablation of Section~\ref{ssec:gap} shows that the functional form accounts for at most about 10\% of the optimum's KL and that re-solving a fixed budget at every step accounts for nearly all of the gap.

\subsection*{Small-system experiments}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Unreproducible headline.} An earlier version reported ``17.9\% vs.\ 53.2\% loop rate at matched KL$\approx0.24$'', which could not be reproduced from its saved artifact: the saved check targeted a different operating point than the one cited. The comparison is now computed and saved for every grid point.
  \item \textbf{Mismatched $\varepsilon$.} Local and hazard-informed control were called with a one-step $\varepsilon$ read directly off the grid used for the optimum's sequence-level target, so the mechanisms were compared at different operating points. All are now matched at the same sequence-level loop probability.
  \item \textbf{Ignored infeasibility flag.} The one-step solver's structural-infeasibility flag was computed but never checked; it is now tracked and reported.
  \item \textbf{Non-absorbing KL.} The Monte Carlo simulator kept charging KL for the full horizon after a trajectory had looped; KL is now absorbing at the loop, matching the exact derivation.
  \item \textbf{Approximate matching.} The equal-distortion table used a Monte Carlo simulator (2000 trajectories per point) and the closest point on a 60-point grid of per-step budgets, so its matched KL differed from the target in the third decimal and its loop probabilities carried sampling noise. The simulator is removed and the budgets are solved exactly; the exact local loop probabilities (44.5, 45.2, 42.1, 25.4, 7.4\%) replace the earlier simulated ones (43.9, 44.2, 41.1, 24.9, 9.6\%), and the exact KL ceiling is $0.614985$ bits per step instead of an estimated $\approx0.613$.
  \item \textbf{Base of the exponent.} The derivation mixed $2^{-\lambda}$ and $e^{-\lambda}$; it uses base 2 throughout, and the closed-form identity holds to $<10^{-14}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Baseline reimplementations}
\textbf{FSD-style.} Three versions were implemented. The first combined the model and the anti-LM in logit space with a longest-available-order heuristic over the full vocabulary. The second used the paper's probability-space scoring over the top-$k$ candidates, with fixed normalized interpolation weights $\beta^{N-k}$ (about $0.30/0.33/0.37$ for orders $1/2/3$ at $N{=}3$, $\beta{=}0.9$) and $\text{count}/\text{total}$ at every order. The official anti-LM instead allocates residual mass recursively and smooths higher orders by $\text{count}/(\text{total}{+}1)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:fsd}). The third version, checked against the official function, is the one reported. Between the second and the third version the primary-model loop count moved from 2/150 to 3/150, non-fiction from 16.7\% to 16.0\%, GPT-2 mcNLL from 2.122 to 2.178 (0.0\% loops in both), OLMo KL from 3.188 to 3.194 bits, Mistral loops from 0/50 to 1/50 and KL from 4.252 to 4.459 bits, common-KL KL from 2.184 to 2.148 bits, and the spelling-error rate from 2.59\% to 2.29\%; the first-to-second version change moved the primary-model loop rate from 0.0\% to 1.3\%.

\textbf{LZ Penalty.} An earlier version scored a single identified match instead of every candidate, used a different formula for non-matching tokens, and had the opposite sign convention. The ported version, checked against the reference at the pinned commit, moved the primary-model KL to 0.186 bits, non-fiction loops from 0.0\% to 2.0\%, GPT-2 loops from 25.0\% to 8.0\%, Mistral loops from 6.0\% to 2.0\%, and the spelling-error rate from 1.58\% to 0.75\%. Its history handling still differs from the reference (Appendix~\ref{app:lz}).

\subsection*{Real-model KL analysis}
An earlier version of Section~\ref{ssec:eventlevel} pooled all prompts into a single reference loop rate and reported that every decoder spends ``at least 50 times'' the event-level term. Pooling prompts lowers the bound relative to the prompt-conditional one (joint convexity of $d_{\mathrm{Ber}}$) and treats estimated quantities as exact, so it overstated how much distortion could be avoided. The analysis now conditions on the prompt, keeps event counts and path KL together, resamples whole prompts, and reports plug-in estimates with intervals.

\subsection*{Hazard estimator}
The first rollout estimator (i) detected the loop on the continuation alone, discarding the earlier context, so that a cycle begun in the context and completed in the rollout was missed, which is the case a hazard estimate must capture; and (ii) left-padded variable-length contexts with a real token id and no attention mask, which changes the next-token distribution of the model. Its earlier null result (mean hazard 0.027 for risky and 0.028 for novel candidates, $n{=}156$ and $1644$, and no separation in the multi-step variant) therefore came from an estimator that did not measure the quantity it was named for and is not used. Both defects are fixed (Appendix~\ref{sec:hazard-transfer}).

\subsection*{Pilot human evaluation and release}
The pilot's FSD-style passages came from the intermediate FSD-style version, which an earlier draft described inconsistently in two places; Appendix~\ref{sec:human-eval} identifies the version by matching the passages against saved runs. A public snapshot of the repository taken before the final changes contained the earlier FSD-style code and lacked the tests and the new result files; the release now contains the code, tests, and results for every table (Appendix~\ref{sec:reproducibility}).
